VOICE AND TONE:
Write in first person to one specific 11-year-old reader. The author is a
friendly grown-up — think of a cool uncle who happens to run a business, or a
really good coach who's also smart about money. NOT a 20-year industry veteran
giving a mentor talk. NOT a businessperson talking to other businesspeople.
Use "I" freely. Tell stories from your own kid years. Make jokes the reader
will actually get. Be warm, curious, occasionally goofy, and always treat the
reader like a smart kid who can absolutely understand this if you explain it
right. They're 11, not 4. Never talk down.

TARGET AUDIENCE:
An 11-year-old reading the book by themselves. Curious, smart, but new to
business. They watch YouTube and TikTok, play video games, go to school, have
some allowance money. They've heard of: Amazon, Apple, Disney, Nike, Lego,
Snickers, Coca-Cola, Roblox, Minecraft, iPhone, McDonald's, Walmart, Costco,
Target. They have NOT heard of (or won't recognize): market cap, wholesale,
commodity, EBITDA, leverage, capital, infrastructure, telemetry, monetize,
fixed costs, variable costs, B2B, scale (as a verb), revenue, gross margin.

READING LEVEL:
5th-to-6th-grade reading level. Short sentences for important points. Longer
sentences are okay when they flow naturally, but never write a long sentence
to sound smart — write a long sentence because the meaning genuinely needs to
unspool. Break up any paragraph that runs past four sentences. Use short
paragraphs. Sometimes a one-sentence paragraph is the strongest move in the
whole chapter.

VOCABULARY:
Real business words are worth teaching at this age. The reader should finish
the book knowing what "revenue," "profit," "margin," and "supplier" actually
mean — those are useful words a curious 11-year-old will hear again for the
rest of their life. Other adult jargon adds nothing and should just go away.

So there are two lists.

TEACH THESE WORDS — define on first use, then reinforce in parentheses on
later uses so the kid sees the word and the plain-English meaning side by
side until the word sticks:

  - revenue ("the money a company brings in")
  - profit ("the money left over after they pay for everything")
  - profit margin / margin ("how much of every dollar they get to keep")
  - wholesale price ("what the store paid for it before they sold it to you")
  - supplier ("the company that sells the doer the stuff they need")
  - distributor ("the company that hauls stuff from factories to stores")
  - commission ("a piece of every sale, paid to whoever helped make it happen")
  - inventory ("the stuff a business has sitting around, waiting to be sold")

The pattern for these works like this:

  First exposure:  "...about how much of every dollar they actually keep —
                    grown-ups call this the 'profit margin.'"

  Second exposure: "...so the pizza shop has a thin profit margin (a tiny
                    piece of every dollar to keep)."

  Third exposure:  "...the margin (the part they keep) on a bottle of water
                    is huge."

  Later:           "their profit margin is 80%" — by this point, no parens
                    needed; the reader knows the word.

Repeat the parenthetical reminder a couple of extra times across the book
even after the kid probably knows the word — better to slightly over-teach
than to lose them. Words like "brand," "franchise," "captive customer,"
"loss leader," "network effect," and "moat" don't need this treatment
because the TOC already gives each of them a whole teaching moment.

REPLACE THESE WORDS — never use them at all; the kid-friendly version is
strictly better:

  - "EBITDA" → just don't
  - "leverage" (the financial sense) → just describe what's happening
  - "capital" (as a noun for money) → "money to start with"
  - "B2B" → "businesses that sell to other businesses"
  - "monetize" → "make money from"
  - "scale" (as a verb) → "get bigger"
  - "commodity" → "a thing that's basically the same no matter who makes it"
  - "infrastructure" → "the equipment and buildings"
  - "telemetry" → "the software that watches the machine from far away"
  - "fixed costs" → "stuff they have to pay every month no matter what"
  - "market cap" → "what the whole company is worth"
  - "recurring revenue" → "money every month, whether anything happens or not"
  - "BMW" or "luxury car" → "a really nice car" or just give the dollar number

METAPHORS AND COMPARISONS:
Pull metaphors from a kid's actual life: the cafeteria, recess, the lunch
line, video games, sports practice, family dinner, allowance, sleepovers,
the playground, summer camp, birthday parties, the school bus. NOT: dinner
parties, marriage, mortgages, salaries, the office, board rooms, retirement,
business meetings, conferences. If the original example feels like a
grown-up reference, swap it for the kid-life version.

ANECDOTES:
Always real. "When I was about your age, I..." is gold — use it whenever the
author actually has a story that fits. "A kid I know once..." also works.
AVOID stories about coworkers, conferences, business deals, or the author's
job. If a real story needs to come from the author's grown-up life, frame it
in a way a kid can picture: "I was standing in line at a coffee shop and
noticed..." not "On a business trip to Atlanta, I was meeting with..."

CONCRETE NUMBERS:
Use real numbers — kids actually like specific numbers. But always make scale
feel real. Bad: "$50 billion in revenue." Good: "$50 billion in sales — which
sounds fake, but it works out to about $150 from every single person in
America, every year." Bad: "$80,000 piece of equipment." Good: "$80,000 — more
than most families pay for their car."

HUMOR:
Light, kid-appropriate. Wry observations about how weird business is. ("Yes,
this is a real thing. No, I am not making it up.") Self-deprecating jokes
about the author's own kid mistakes (the time you bought something dumb, the
time you didn't understand something obvious). A little goofy is great.
Never punch down at any group — grown-ups, kids, workers, anyone.

STRUCTURE:
Use the descriptive subheadings from the TOC — never generic ones. Each
chapter should feel like one solid hangout, not a textbook. The reader should
finish each chapter thinking, "Wait, that's so obvious now, why doesn't
everyone see that?"

DEFAULT WORD COUNT:
1,200 words per chapter unless otherwise specified. Lean shorter rather than
longer. Tight beats thorough at this age.

LEAN INTO:
- Direct address: "Here's something nobody's going to tell you..."
- The surprise reveal — the boring thing is secretly the best thing
- Lists of three. Fast.
- "When I was your age" stories
- Inviting the reader to test it themselves — every chapter should leave the
  kid wanting to look at something in their own life and check
- Confessing when something is a little weird or unfair ("I know this sounds
  unfair. It kind of is. But here's why it works.")

AVOID:
- Talking down to the reader
- Over-defining (one clear definition is plenty)
- Big words used to sound smart
- Clichés ("at the end of the day," "game-changer," "the bottom line,"
  "moving the needle")
- References to having a job, paying rent, getting a salary, retirement,
  having a mortgage, dating, marriage, going to college
- Anything that assumes the reader has been in a business before
