VOICE AND TONE:
Write primarily in the second person. The reader is the protagonist: a real
estate development veteran who has read hundreds of pay applications from the
owner's side but is holding the certifier's pen for the first time. The
narrator is a senior practitioner coaching the reader through a live decision,
calm, precise, and unsentimental. First person from the coach is fine in small
doses ("I have watched reps freeze right here"), but the center of gravity is
"you," in the seat, deciding. This is a flight simulator, not a memoir. The
register is a working professional walking a peer through a hard call, not a
mentor telling stories over coffee.

TARGET AUDIENCE:
Industry insiders. Assume a reader fluent in real estate development and
construction finance. Do not explain, from scratch, terms a developer already
owns: schedule of values, draw, pay application, retainage, GC, sub, OAC
meeting, general conditions, mobilization. Use them directly. What IS new to
this reader, and must be taught precisely, is the North Carolina lien and
payment statutory regime and the money-control mechanics: the lien on funds,
double-payment liability, the subrogation nuance, waiver rhythm, joint versus
direct checks, the disburser's limits. Teach those carefully; assume the rest.

READING LEVEL:
Professional. Precision beats simplicity. Never dumb down a statute or round
off a mechanic to make it easier; the accuracy is the value. Use short,
declarative sentences for the rule, the move, and the script beats. Use longer
sentences for the reasoning and the scene. Plain and hard, never academic and
never padded.

THE SCENARIO IS A SIMULATOR, NOT A TRUE STORY:
The running scenario, a 90-unit North Carolina condominium build and a
litigious, judgment-proof GC, is a deliberate composite built for rehearsal.
Present it as exactly that. Do NOT invent real-sounding war stories, do NOT
claim events actually happened, and do NOT name any real person, project,
firm, GC, or sub. The realism comes from procedural and statutory accuracy,
not from pretending the drill is a documented case. When a chapter needs a
concrete number (a draw amount, a claimed lien figure), use the figures the
TOC supplies and keep them internal to the simulation.

METAPHORS:
Use them sparingly and only when they genuinely illuminate. The flight
simulator is the one governing metaphor for the book; an opening analogy is
welcome where the TOC calls for one (as in the first chapter), but most
chapters should open on the concrete: the document landing, the GC's actual
words, the statutory fact. Do not force an everyday-life metaphor into every
chapter. A forced metaphor dilutes the rehearsal and reads as filler.

SCRIPT BEATS:
Many chapters include the actual sentences to say in the seat. Set them off
clearly and keep them sayable: short, plain, the way a real person talks under
pressure, not lawyerly. Just as important, honor the "where to stop talking"
discipline the TOC calls for. After the line that holds the position, stop.
Do not have the narrator keep negotiating against himself on the page.

NORTH CAROLINA SPECIFICITY:
Cite statutes and authorities exactly as the TOC bullets provide them (section
numbers, effective dates, case names). Never invent a statute number, a
holding, or a deadline. If the prose would need a specific legal detail the
TOC bullet does not supply, stay at the level of generality the bullet
supports rather than fabricating precision. Accuracy on the law is
non-negotiable; a wrong section number is worse than no section number.

CONFIRM-WITH-COUNSEL POSTURE:
The book is rehearsal material, not legal advice, and most chapters close with
a concrete "confirm with counsel" beat. Render it once per chapter and make it
specific (name exactly what to confirm: the service requirements of the
notice, the contract's withholding grounds, the joint-check doctrine's NC
status). Do not turn it into repeated boilerplate, and never let the
disclaimer soften the decisiveness of the drill. The reader should leave
knowing the move and knowing precisely which piece a lawyer must bless.

HUMOR:
Mostly out. The stakes are personal liability, wrongful-payment exposure, and
clouded unit titles. A spare, dry line is fine where it lands naturally;
self-aware observation about the absurdity of a GC's move is fine. Do not
reach for levity, and never undercut a high-stakes beat with a joke.

WORD COUNT:
The per-chapter word target comes from the TOC and is supplied in the
generation prompt. Write to that target and no other; do not aim for any
different length. Brevity is a feature of this book, not a shortfall. Do not
pad to fill space: if the decision is made and the paper is described, the
chapter is done. A tight chapter that lands the drill beats a long one that
restates it.

STRUCTURE:
Every chapter follows the same fixed four-beat spine, because the repetition
is the training method, not a default to deviate from: (1) the scene and the
counterparty's move, in his own voice; (2) both wrong answers, the soft flinch
and the aggressive flinch; (3) the correct seat and the concrete move, with
script beats; (4) how you know you held it, the paper and the debrief. Use the
scenario-specific subheadings the TOC provides; do not substitute generic ones
("Section 1") and do not vary the spine from chapter to chapter. Consistency
across chapters is the point.
