# Construction-Phase Money Control: Practice Scenarios

## Source brief for deep-research session and table-of-contents generation

This document is the grounding material for a short practice book of 15 to 20 case-study scenarios. The book exists to let one person role-play the decisions that arise when an owner's representative runs the money-control function on a construction project: pay-application certification, change orders, lien-waiver monitoring, and funds control against a contractor with a documented pattern of abusing the draw process. Each scenario is a rehearsable decision point with a right move, a wrong move in two directions, and a way to tell which one you made.

Use this brief to inform the research session. The "Scenario seeds" section near the end is the direct input for the table-of-contents generator: roughly twenty discrete situations, each tagged with the skill it trains and the reflex it tests.

---

## 1. Who the protagonist is, and why this book is needed

The protagonist is an experienced multifamily development professional who has spent a decade on the development side: site work, entitlements, design oversight, financial modeling, deal structure. He has read hundreds of pay applications from the owner-development seat. He has never once held the approver's pen. He has never written a directive, never been the primary certifier of a pay app, never run the withholding posture against a contractor trying to defeat it.

He is now taking that function on directly for the first time, as the lead on his firm's debut private construction-management engagement, and he is learning it live against the worst possible teacher: a general contractor specifically known to front-load draws and stiff lower-tier subcontractors. The book is his flight simulator. The goal is to make the judgment under fire automatic before the live cycles, so that on the job he is learning to refine a posture he already rehearsed, not improvising it for the first time with real money and his firm's reputation on the line.

A second, structural reason the book matters: the money-control function is the exact capability gap that otherwise caps his leverage in his broader business. His construction partner currently owns this whole half of the work by default because the protagonist cannot yet run it himself. Closing this gap converts him from dependent to capable. So the scenarios are not just risk drills; they are the curriculum for a gating professional skill.

---

## 2. The deal context (the standing fact pattern for every scenario)

Hold this cast and these facts constant across scenarios unless a scenario deliberately varies them. Consistency makes the book role-play-ready rather than a loose anthology.

- **The project:** a 90-unit condominium build. Jurisdiction is North Carolina, which matters because the lien and waiver law is state-specific and diverges sharply from the statutory-form states.
- **The owner ("the principal"):** cost-focused, sophisticated, weighs every fee against the total job number, and will feel pressure to keep the contractor happy and the schedule moving.
- **The owner's internal project manager:** competent on the build, stretched thin by a personal crisis, with no bandwidth to also fight the contractor over money. His dignity and his role have to be preserved, not displaced.
- **The owner's skeptic (a second principal or partner):** asks the structural, duplication-probing questions, wants to know exactly what the money-control function buys that monitoring alone does not.
- **The general contractor ("the GC"):** litigious, effectively judgment-proof, with a known pattern across prior jobs of front-loading draws, billing ahead of work in place, and failing to pay lower-tier subs and suppliers, leaving prior owners to finance him, pay twice, and absorb six-figure holes long after closeout.
- **The protagonist's two service tiers:** "Option A" is monitoring only (the owner's superintendent: verifies the job is built to code, plan, and schedule). "Option B" adds control of the money (the owner's superintendent and project manager: pay-app certification, draw control, change-order authority, withholding posture, lower-tier payment verification). The scenarios in this book live almost entirely in Option B, because that is the function being learned and the function priced for exactly this contractor.

---

## 3. The one organizing principle

Every scenario in the book is, underneath, a variation on a single rule:

**Cash must never get ahead of verified, lien-released work in place.**

Front-loading is how cash gets ahead of work. Weak percent-complete verification is how cash gets ahead of work. Stored-materials games, premature retainage release, and unverified change orders are all the same failure in different costumes. The lien-waiver and funds-control machinery is what proves the work in place was also paid for down the tiers, so the cash you release does not come back as a second bill. A reader who internalizes this one sentence has the spine of the whole book; the scenarios are the muscle.

---

## 4. The two reflexes the book trains against

The protagonist has a documented dual reflex, and good scenarios test both directions, because his seat is narrower than either flinch.

- **The under-claim reflex:** giving away value, authority, or his own seat. In money control this looks like rubber-stamping a pay app to avoid conflict, releasing a draw to keep the peace, accepting the GC's percentages on faith, volunteering a cheaper service tier when no real budget wall exists, or paying his partner out of his own fee because he feels green.
- **The over-reach reflex:** picking up liability that is not his to hold. In money control this looks like paying subcontractors directly in a way that assumes means-and-methods responsibility, becoming the check-writer, or directing the work in ways that convert him from owner's rep into de facto contractor.

The correct seat: **he controls and verifies the release of funds; the GC builds; the owner (or a funds-control agent) funds.** Under-claim gives away protection; over-reach gives away liability protection. Same flinch, opposite directions. Scenarios should be written so the obvious wrong answer is sometimes too soft and sometimes too aggressive, forcing the reader to find the narrow correct seat rather than just "be tougher."

## 5. Domain knowledge to ground the research

The research session should expand and verify the following. This is the technical substance the scenarios dramatize.

### 5.1 Pay-application approval pitfalls, ranked by what actually causes loss

1. **The schedule of values (SOV), set before pay app one.** Front-loading is the signature abuse: loading mobilization, general conditions, and early trades so billings run ahead of work in place; by mid-job the GC has collected far more than built, and a walk-off leaves the money gone. It is a one-shot decision made before any cycles of experience and it governs the whole job. Mastery: refuse an unbalanced SOV; push general conditions and mobilization to defensible levels; make every line map to a measurable physical milestone; eliminate unverifiable lump allowances.
2. **Lien waivers and lower-tier payment.** The literal mechanism behind the double-payment holes. Pay the GC, the GC stiffs subs and suppliers, they lien the property, the owner pays twice. Mastery: never release a draw without conditional waivers for the current amount plus unconditional waivers proving the prior draw reached subs and suppliers down to the lower tiers, not merely the GC's own waiver.
3. **Percent-complete verification.** The recurring judgment that is the certifier's actual job. Is the billed percentage truly in place? Desk approval is how owners get fleeced. Mastery: walk the work against the SOV every cycle; never certify a percentage you cannot verify; treat every GC figure as aggressive until proven. This is the skill that improves fastest with reps.
4. **Stored materials.** Billing for materials not installed, sometimes not on site, sometimes never delivered. Mastery: no payment without proof of purchase, evidence of proper on-site or bonded off-site storage, insurance, and ideally a bill of sale moving title to the owner. Off-site claims from this GC get extra scrutiny.
5. **Retainage.** The owner's structural leverage and easy to give away to keep the peace. Mastery: hold the contracted percentage; resist mid-job early-release requests; tie final release to completed punch and full lien releases.

### 5.2 Change-order pitfalls (contract command, not money-in-place)

6. **Base scope billed as a change.** Recharacterizing work already in the drawings and specs as new scope. Mastery: test every change order against "was this in the contract documents," which requires actually knowing them cold.
7. **Unauthorized and directed work.** Work proceeds on a verbal or field directive, then the change order arrives after the money is effectively committed. Mastery: nothing changes without written authorization before the work happens; "we already did it" never becomes pricing leverage.
8. **Change-order pricing and time, bundled.** Inflated markups and time-and-materials padding on cost; reflexive schedule extensions that erode the liquidated-damages position. Mastery: pre-agreed markup caps and T&M rules in the contract; separate cost from time on every change order so days do not ride in free with the dollars.

### 5.3 North Carolina lien and waiver law (jurisdiction-specific, verify and expand)

- **Lien agent system (LiensNC).** NC requires designation of a lien agent when total improvements reach roughly $40,000 or more, designated by the time of permit issuance or first improvement contract (single-family owner-occupied exemption aside). Potential claimants file a Notice to Lien Agent through the LiensNC portal, generally within 15 days of first furnishing. The project's Notice-to-Lien-Agent filings are the master roster of who could lien the project. A claim of lien on real property is filed within 120 days of last furnishing and enforced by suit within 180 days.
- **No statutory waiver form.** NC is not among the small set of states that regulate waiver form and content, so the parties draft their own language. That is a liability if the forms are loose and an advantage if they are drafted tight. Forms should be papered by NC construction counsel.
- **Interim-waiver limit (Chapter 22B, effective 2022).** A waiver required as a condition of an interim or progress payment is enforceable only to the extent of the payment actually received, even if its language reaches further. A conditional-on-payment form is both safer and harder to weaponize.
- **No-lien-clause prohibition (44A-12(f)).** An agreement to waive lien rights made in anticipation of and in consideration for the contract award is against public policy and unenforceable. Subordination and release are not prohibited.
- **The subrogation nuance (44A-23) (the trap that decides whether the whole scheme protects the owner).** A waiver signed by the GC waives a subcontractor's ability to enforce the GC's lien on the real property through subrogation, but it does NOT affect the sub's claim of lien on funds, nor the sub's real-property lien for wrongful payment. A clean stack of GC waivers is therefore insufficient on its own.
- **Lien on funds and wrongful payment.** A sub can serve a Notice of Claim of Lien Upon Funds. If the owner (or the disbursing agent) pays the GC after receiving that notice, it is a wrongful payment and the owner can be liable to pay twice. The disbursement process must screen for funds-lien notices before every release, which is distinct from a title-lien screen.
- **Notice of Contract / Notice of Subcontract regime.** If the GC posts and files a Notice of Contract at the jobsite, lower-tier subs must file Notices of Subcontract to preserve subrogation rights, and a five-day notice-of-payment cadence applies. On a job whose entire risk is invisible lower-tier nonpayment, forcing that visibility early is a deliberate owner-side tool.
- **Attorney closings.** NC requires attorney-conducted real estate closings; the closing attorney already clears LiensNC for clear title at closeout. The money-control regime runs that same reconciliation every draw instead of once.

### 5.4 The waiver-monitoring system

- **The rhythm: conditional current, unconditional prior, one cycle lag.** Each cycle, collect conditional progress waivers (effective only on actual receipt of payment) from the GC and every lower-tier party for the current requested amount. Before releasing the current draw, collect unconditional waivers for the prior draw proving last cycle's money reached the lower tiers. Never demand an unconditional waiver before payment; never fund the next dollar until the prior dollar is documented all the way down.
- **Roster reconciliation.** Pull the LiensNC Notice-to-Lien-Agent list each cycle and reconcile against the waiver log. Anyone who filed a notice but has not given an unconditional waiver for the prior draw is a withhold.
- **Funds-lien screen.** Independently screen for any Notice of Claim of Lien Upon Funds before each release; withhold the claimed amount if one arrives.

### 5.5 Joint checks and direct disbursement

- **Conjunctive vs disjunctive.** A check payable to "GC and Sub" requires both endorsements and cannot be negotiated on one signature (the conjunctive form is the hard gate). A check payable to "GC or Sub" can be cashed by either alone and protects nothing. Always conjunctive.
- **The endorse-it-over trap.** A conjunctive check still fails if the GC carries it to the sub, gets the endorsement on a promise to pay "next week," and deposits the whole thing. The gate is defeated when the sub hands back its leverage. Fix: controlled delivery (exchange at an OAC meeting or through the disbursing agent, sub endorses and receives its portion in the same moment), or better, direct disbursement.
- **Direct disbursement.** Escrow cuts a separate check to the at-risk sub for the sub's portion and a separate check to the GC for the balance; the GC never touches the sub's money. Requires knowing subcontract values, which a devious GC resists, because opacity is the scheme.
- **Disclosure: subcontracts vs sworn statement.** You do not need the subcontracts. You need the sub roster and per-cycle amounts owed and paid, obtainable through a contractor's sworn statement reconciled against the named, dollar-specific lien waivers. Framing the ask as "sworn statement plus reconciled waivers" rather than "show me your subcontracts" gets the needed visibility and is far harder to paint as unreasonable.
- **Lien-clearing bonus.** A sub's endorsement of a joint check (or receipt of a direct check) is generally treated as payment to that sub for lien purposes up to that amount, retiring the lien-on-funds and wrongful-payment exposure on the dollars that provably landed. Confirm the precise NC application with counsel.

### 5.6 The title company's real role and its limits

A title company acting as construction disburser holds the money, collects waivers, and at closing insures clear title. It does NOT verify work in place, and unless explicitly instructed it may not screen for funds-lien notices. It works off the prime contract and the GC's schedule of values and does not inherently need the subcontracts; subcontract-level visibility is forced by the control regime (direct or joint disbursement), not by the title company's involvement. Treat the title company as "holds the money and the log and will not release without a clean reconciliation," and keep the judgment layer with the owner's rep.

### 5.7 Leverage, timing, and the executed contract

- The executed owner-GC contract sets the ceiling on everything. Funds control, sworn statements, and direct disbursement must be rights the owner already holds or trades for; they cannot be imposed on a litigious GC that has no obligation to accept them.
- Pre-signing, leverage is at its peak and these terms are conditions of award. Post-signing without a hook, direct disbursement is a low-probability ask unless traded for value (faster payment timing, reduced early-cycle retainage, streamlined change orders) or unless a default-triggered or lien-triggered direct-payment right already exists in the contract.
- The achievable 80 percent post-signing is conditioning each draw on documentation: contractor's sworn statement, current conditional and prior unconditional waivers, and lower-tier payment evidence, much of it grounded in clauses the GC already signed (lien-waiver-as-condition-of-payment, reasonable evidence of sub payment, schedule of values). Direct disbursement is the hard last 20 percent.
- The GC's response to the modest ask (a clean contractor provides sworn statements without blinking) is itself diagnostic. Resistance confirms the problem the control regime exists to contain.

### 5.8 Partial certification

Certifying less than the applied-for amount is routine, not exceptional, and is built into standard contract machinery (the certifier may certify a different amount and withhold for stated cause). Doing it defensibly against a litigious GC requires: certify by line item at verified percentages and let the total fall out (never a blanket lump-sum haircut, which lets the GC apply released money to the lines you doubted); document each withheld amount by reason in separate buckets (disputed percent-complete, undocumented stored materials, missing lower-tier waivers or a funds-lien notice, defective work), because each releases on different conditions; track retainage and the uncertified hold as two distinct mechanisms; certify against the contract's specific withholding grounds and hit any notice-of-reasons requirement, because a procedurally defective withholding becomes the GC's prompt-payment or breach claim. The mechanic is simple; doing it cleanly against a contractor hunting for a procedural opening is the skill.

## 6. The scenario template (the research session should follow this for each case study)

Each case study should be written so it can be run as a role-play. Keep this structure consistent across all of them:

1. **Setup.** The specific facts: the dollars, the line items, the cast members present, the cycle number, the contract posture (signed or not, and what it grants).
2. **The move.** What the counterparty does or says, in their own voice. The pressure, the probe, the game.
3. **The trap, both directions.** The under-claim wrong answer and the over-reach wrong answer, named explicitly, so the reader sees both flinches.
4. **The correct posture and the specific move.** The narrow correct seat and the concrete action.
5. **Script beats.** The actual sentences to say, and where to stop talking. Short, deliverable lines, not paragraphs.
6. **How you know you held it.** Observable success markers. What the GC does next if you got it right; what the paper looks like.
7. **The law and contract hooks.** Which NC provisions or contract clauses govern, and where to confirm with counsel.
8. **Debrief questions.** Three or four self-review prompts for after the role-play: where did I concede, was it scoped or reflexive, what would I cut on the tape, what do I do differently next cycle.

## 7. Scenario seeds (direct input for the table-of-contents generator)

Roughly twenty discrete decision points, each tagged with the core skill and the reflex it tests. Trim or merge to land at 15 to 20 chapters. Sequence them to follow the job's chronology where possible (SOV first, closeout last), because that doubles as a lifecycle walkthrough.

1. **Setting the schedule of values.** The GC submits an SOV loaded toward mobilization and general conditions. Hold the line on a balanced, milestone-mapped SOV before the first dollar moves. (Skill: SOV / front-loading. Reflex: under-claim.)
2. **First pay app, percent-complete inflated.** A line bills 50 percent complete; the field shows materially less. Certify only what is verified. (Skill: percent-complete verification. Reflex: under-claim.)
3. **Off-site stored materials with no proof.** A large stored-materials line for materials not on site and not bonded. Withhold pending documentation. (Skill: stored materials. Reflex: under-claim.)
4. **Mid-job retainage release request.** The GC pushes to cut or release retainage citing cash flow. Hold the contracted percentage. (Skill: retainage. Reflex: under-claim.)
5. **A lien-on-funds notice arrives before a draw.** A second-tier sub serves a Notice of Claim of Lien Upon Funds days before a scheduled release. Withhold the claimed amount or face double payment. (Skill: funds-lien screen, wrongful payment. Reflex: under-claim.)
6. **The broad unconditional interim waiver.** The GC offers a sweeping unconditional waiver to satisfy the cycle. Recognize the form trap and the NC interim-waiver limit; require the correct conditional-current form. (Skill: waiver form / NC 22B-5. Reflex: over-reach risk if you rely on the wrong instrument.)
7. **Roster reconciliation gap.** A claimant on the LiensNC notice list has given no unconditional waiver for the prior draw. Withhold until reconciled. (Skill: roster reconciliation. Reflex: under-claim.)
8. **"Or" check pressure.** The GC asks for a disjunctive joint check for convenience. Hold for conjunctive. (Skill: joint-check construction. Reflex: under-claim.)
9. **The endorse-it-over move.** The GC wants to carry a conjunctive joint check to the sub to collect the endorsement. Insist on controlled delivery or direct disbursement. (Skill: joint-check delivery. Reflex: under-claim.)
10. **Pushing for direct disbursement post-signing.** The contract is signed without a funds-control hook; you seek the sworn-statement-plus-reconciled-waiver regime, and the GC resists subcontract disclosure. Frame the narrower ask; read the resistance. (Skill: disclosure framing, leverage. Reflex: both.)
11. **Base scope billed as a change order.** A change order recharacterizes work already in the contract documents as new scope. Test it against the drawings and specs. (Skill: change-order scope. Reflex: under-claim.)
12. **Field-directed work, no written authorization.** Work proceeded on a verbal directive; the change order arrives after the fact. Refuse retroactive pricing leverage. (Skill: directed work / written authorization. Reflex: under-claim.)
13. **Inflated change order with a bundled time extension.** Padded markup plus a reflexive schedule extension. Cap the markup, unbundle cost from time. (Skill: change-order pricing and time. Reflex: under-claim.)
14. **Partial certification.** A 2.0 million application; 1.7 is supported. Certify by line item, document the 0.3 hold by reason, keep retainage separate. (Skill: partial certification. Reflex: both, defensible paper against a litigious GC.)
15. **The owner pressures you to release.** The principal, wanting to keep the GC moving, leans on you to certify a draw you have not verified. Hold the function you were hired to perform. (Skill: holding the seat against the client. Reflex: under-claim, self/other line live.)
16. **The PM overlap.** The owner's stretched PM asks whether you take over pay-app review or merely double-check his. Answer the operational question straight; divide the money function as relief, not replacement. (Skill: role division, answering operational questions. Reflex: under-claim by going passive.)
17. **The GC threatens a slowdown or a claim over a withholding.** A legitimate withholding draws a threat to stop work or file a claim. Hold the procedurally clean hold; do not buy peace with the owner's money. (Skill: withholding under pressure. Reflex: under-claim.)
18. **An unpaid sub calls you directly.** A lower-tier sub, unpaid and about to lien, calls you though you are not in privity. Manage it without picking up means-and-methods liability or becoming the check-writer. (Skill: scope and privity discipline. Reflex: over-reach.)
19. **Negotiating money control into the deal before signing.** The leverage-at-peak version: funds control as a condition of award before the GC holds a signed contract and a mobilized crew. (Skill: pre-signing leverage. Reflex: under-claim.)
20. **The over-reach temptation.** Frustrated by slow lower-tier payment, you consider just paying the subs directly and sorting it out later. Recognize the means-and-methods liability you would assume and stay in the verify-and-release seat. (Skill: the narrow seat. Reflex: over-reach.)

## 8. Notes for the research session

- Verify the North Carolina statutory specifics against current law (Chapter 44A, Chapter 22B section 5, the lien-agent thresholds and deadlines, the subrogation provision in 44A-23, and the UCC joint-check rule). The law section above reflects the state of play as researched but should be confirmed and dated in the finished book, with the standing caveat that the book is practice material, not legal advice, and that forms and disbursement agreements should be papered by NC construction counsel.
- Keep the protagonist's voice consistent: a domain expert in development learning the construction-admin function, not a novice to the industry. The scenarios should never explain construction fundamentals; they should dramatize the specific judgment of holding the approver's pen.
- Where a scenario benefits from it, write the counterparty's lines with real teeth. The GC is good at this. The drills are only useful if the pressure is realistic.
- The book should be short and dense. Each chapter is a single decision point, not a survey. The reader should be able to run one chapter as a five-minute role-play and debrief it in three questions.
