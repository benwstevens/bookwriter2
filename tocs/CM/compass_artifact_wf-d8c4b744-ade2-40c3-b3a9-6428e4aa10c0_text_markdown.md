# Research Dossier & Proposed Table of Contents: A Practice/Role-Play Book on Construction-Phase Money Control for a North Carolina Owner's Representative

## TL;DR
- North Carolina law uniquely rewards the "cash must never get ahead of verified, lien-released work in place" rule: under **N.C. Gen. Stat. § 44A-20(b)**, an owner/disbursing agent who pays after receiving a Notice of Claim of Lien Upon Funds is **personally liable** to the unpaid claimant up to the amount of the wrongful payment — a true double-payment exposure — and under **§ 44A-23(c)** a clean stack of GC lien waivers does NOT extinguish a subcontractor's lien on funds or its real-property lien for wrongful payment.
- All core statutory facts in the project brief are confirmed as current law, with two corrections: (1) the lien-agent threshold is now **$40,000** (raised from $30,000 by **Session Law 2023-108, s. 2(h)**, effective **October 1, 2023** — NOT Session Law 2024-30, which is a DMV/transportation bill that amended the unrelated possessory-lien notice provision § 44A-4(b)); and (2) NC has **no statutory lien-waiver form**, and the private prompt-pay statute (Chapter 22C) does not regulate owner-to-GC payment timing.
- The book can be built around 18 scenarios mapped to verified provisions: § 44A-12 (120-day lien), § 44A-13 (180-day suit), §§ 44A-11.1/11.2 (lien agent, 15-day notice), § 22B-5 (progress-payment waiver limit, eff. 3/1/2022), § 44A-12(f) (no-lien clauses void), § 44A-23 (subrogation/Notice of Contract), Chapter 22C (prompt pay, 1%/month), AIA G702/G703/A201 §9.5.1, and UCC § 25-3-110(d) (joint checks).

## Key Findings

**1. The double-payment trap is statutory and severe.** § 44A-20(b) imposes personal liability on the obligor who pays through a funds-lien notice. This is the spine of the entire book and the legal engine behind the organizing rule.

**2. The "clean GC waiver is not enough" nuance is real and precise.** § 44A-23(c) confirms a GC's lien waiver waives only the sub's *subrogation* right to enforce the GC's real-property lien — not the sub's own lien on funds or its § 44A-20(d) wrongful-payment lien against the owner's property.

**3. Two recent statutory changes matter and must be dated correctly:** § 22B-5 (progress-payment waiver limit, eff. March 1, 2022) and the $40,000 lien-agent threshold (Session Law 2023-108, eff. October 1, 2023). Any draft attributing the threshold change to Session Law 2024-30 is wrong.

## Details

### PART A — North Carolina Lien & Waiver Law (verified against current statute)

**Claim of lien on real property — § 44A-12(b):** must be filed "not later than 120 days after the last furnishing of labor or materials at the site of the improvement by the person claiming the lien." A claim of lien may not be amended (§ 44A-12(d)); a defective one must be cancelled and a new one substituted within the 120-day window, and re-filing forfeits the original priority/relation-back. The lien relates back to the date of first furnishing (§ 44A-10), which can give it priority over a later-recorded deed of trust. Sources: ncleg.gov (official statute); NCBA Young Lawyers Division "Claims of Lien Step-by-Step Guide."

**Enforcement by suit — § 44A-13:** an action to enforce must be commenced within 180 days of last furnishing. This deadline cannot be extended (absent a tolling agreement; see *Charlotte Motor Speedway, Inc. v. Tindall Corp.*, 672 S.E.2d 691) and runs from last furnishing — so it can be as short as ~60 days after the lien is filed if the lien is filed late in the 120-day window.

**Lien on funds — §§ 44A-18, 44A-19, 44A-20:** First-, second-, and third-tier subcontractors and suppliers have a lien on funds owed up the chain, perfected by serving a written Notice of Claim of Lien Upon Funds on the obligor (effective upon receipt, § 44A-18). The lien on funds arises, attaches, and is effective immediately upon first furnishing (§ 44A-18(f)). There is **no 120-day filing deadline** for a lien on funds — it is bounded only by the underlying contract statute of limitations (generally three years, § 1-52(1)). Once served, the statutory cascade is:
- **§ 44A-20(a):** the obligor must retain funds up to the total of the liens noticed.
- **§ 44A-20(b):** if the obligor makes further payments to the party against whose interest the lien is claimed, the obligor is **personally liable** to the claimant up to the amount of the wrongful payments, not exceeding the total claims noticed before payment.
- **§ 44A-20(c):** the obligor who incurs that liability is entitled to reimbursement/indemnification from the party who received the payment (cold comfort against a judgment-proof GC).
- **§ 44A-20(d):** if the obligor is the owner, the claimant gets a lien on the owner's real property to the extent of the owner's personal liability.

**Lien agent / LiensNC — §§ 44A-11.1, 44A-11.2:** Mandatory lien-agent designation now triggers at **$40,000 or more**, raised from $30,000 by **Session Law 2023-108, s. 2(h), effective October 1, 2023** (confirmed by the current statutory text — "forty thousand dollars ($40,000) or more" — and the statute's history line ending "…2017-168, s. 1; 2023-108, s. 2(h)"). **Correction to the project brief's premise:** Session Law 2024-30 did NOT change this threshold; SL 2024-30 (HB 199, a DMV/transportation omnibus, signed July 2, 2024) amended § 44A-4(b) to add "certified mail with electronic tracking" as a notice method for *possessory*-lien enforcement — entirely unrelated to construction lien agents. Designation is required at original-building-permit issuance or, if no permit, at the time the owner first contracts with any person to improve the property. Potential lien claimants must serve a Notice to Lien Agent within **15 days** of first furnishing to preserve lien rights/priority (a contractor or subcontractor must also pass the lien-agent contact information down to lower-tier subs within 3 business days of contracting). Exemption: improvements to an existing owner-occupied single-family residence (and certain accessory structures). **Drafting trap:** subsection (a1) (the manufactured-home leasehold exclusion) still reads "thirty thousand dollars ($30,000)" — an apparent legislative oversight that practitioners should flag. Lien agents must be NC-licensed title insurance companies/agents drawn from the Department of Insurance list (§ 58-26-45). Filing is via the LiensNC.com portal; a missed Notice to Lien Agent is, per NC construction attorneys, the most common way subcontractors lose lien rights.

**§ 22B-5 (progress-payment waiver limit):** Effective **March 1, 2022**, and applies to liens attached on or after that date (created by **Session Law 2021-183 / SB 219**; some sources cite the companion "Session Law 2022-1" — the operative effective-date clause reads "This section becomes effective March 1, 2022, and applies to liens attached on or after that date"). Verbatim text: "Provisions in lien waivers, releases, construction agreements as defined in G.S. 22B-1(f)(1), or design professional agreements as defined in G.S. 22B-1(f)(5) purporting to require a promisor to submit a waiver or release of liens or claims as a condition of receiving interim or progress payments due from a promisee under a construction agreement or design professional agreement are void and unenforceable unless limited to the specific interim or progress payment actually received by the promisor in exchange for the lien waiver." Two carve-outs: (1) waivers/releases associated with final payment, and (2) agreements to settle/compromise disputed claims already identified by the claimant in writing. Practical effect: a $10,000 progress-payment waiver on a $100,000 contract does not waive lien rights for the $90,000 balance, and writing "unconditional" on the form does not override the rule. No 2024 or 2025 amendment to § 22B-5 was found.

**§ 44A-12(f) (no-lien clauses void):** "An agreement to waive the right to file a claim of lien on real property … or an agreement to waive the right to serve a notice of claim of lien upon funds … which agreement is in anticipation of and in consideration for the awarding of any contract … is against public policy and is unenforceable." It does not prohibit subordination or post-award release. Note the limiting phrase "in anticipation of and in consideration for the awarding of any contract" — once the project begins, a contractor may waive lien rights going forward.

**§ 44A-23 (subrogation / Notice of Contract regime):** First-, second-, and third-tier subs enforce the GC's real-property lien by subrogation (§ 44A-23(a), (b)). **§ 44A-23(c) — the critical nuance:** a lien waiver signed by the GC before the sub perfects its claim "waives the subcontractor's right to enforce the contractor's claim of lien on real property, but does not affect the subcontractor's right to a claim of lien on funds or the subcontractor's right to a claim of lien on real property allowed under G.S. 44A-20(d)." So a clean stack of GC waivers extinguishes only the subrogation path — not the sub's lien on funds or its wrongful-payment lien against the owner. **Notice of Contract / Notice of Subcontract:** the owner or GC may, within 30 days following permit issuance or contract award (whichever is later), post (adjacent to the permit) and file with the clerk of superior court a Notice of Contract; second/third-tier subs must then serve a Notice of Subcontract on the GC to preserve subrogation. There is no fixed deadline to serve the Notice of Subcontract, but the GC need only notify the sub of payments made *after* receipt. **The 5-day cadence:** once a Notice of Subcontract is served, if the GC serves written notice of each subsequent payment within 5 days following that payment, the lower-tier sub loses subrogation rights as to those payments; if the GC fails to give timely notice, the sub retains subrogation. Case note: *Wachovia Bank Nat. Ass'n v. Superior Constr. Corp.*, 213 N.C. App. 341 (2011) — a lien waiver does not change the date of first furnishing and merely precludes the contractor from asserting a lien for amounts already paid.

**Attorney-conducted closings:** NC requires a licensed NC attorney to supervise residential real-estate closings (Authorized Practice Advisory Opinion 2002-1, expressly preserved in G.S. 10B-134.25). Functions reserved to attorneys include abstracting/opining on title, drafting deeds and deeds of trust, and advising on legal rights. Title insurance can issue only after a title opinion from a licensed NC attorney who is not the insurer's employee (§ 58-26-1). **§ 44A-11.2** deems the closing attorney to have satisfied the professional obligation to check LiensNC notices if the attorney requests copies of the notices/renewals/cancellations from the lien agent no more than 5 business days before recording a deed or deed of trust. This is the mechanism by which clear title is delivered at each condo-unit closeout: the closing attorney clears LiensNC and confirms all potential liens are waived or addressed.

**No statutory waiver form:** NC has **no statutory lien-waiver form**, unlike the **12 statutory-form states — Arizona, California, Florida, Georgia, Massachusetts, Michigan, Mississippi, Missouri, Nevada, Texas, Utah, and Wyoming** (per Bradley and Procore guidance; the other 38 states, including NC, do not prescribe form/content). NC parties draft their own; conditional and unconditional waivers are both recognized, but § 22B-5 overrides an "unconditional" label as to unpaid progress amounts. Notarization is **not** required in NC (by contrast, only Mississippi and Wyoming require notarized waivers; Texas dropped its notarization requirement for projects on or after January 1, 2022).

### PART B — Joint Check & UCC Rules

**UCC § 25-3-110(d) (NC's adoption of UCC 3-110(d)):** An instrument payable to two or more persons not alternatively ("A and B") may be negotiated, enforced, or discharged only by all of them acting jointly (requires both endorsements — a conjunctive check); an instrument payable alternatively ("A or B") may be negotiated by any one payee (disjunctive). Ambiguous multi-payee designations (names stacked with no connector) are treated as payable alternatively — i.e., any single payee may negotiate. Conversion exposure under § 25-3-420 arises if a depositary bank takes a conjunctive check on one payee's endorsement only.

**Joint-check rule / doctrine:** Under the joint-check rule, when an owner/GC makes a sub and its supplier (or lower-tier sub) co-payees and no agreement governs allocation, the co-payee who endorses the check is presumed to have received all sums owed to it up to the amount of the check — even if it actually received less — thereby waiving lien/bond claims for that amount. The doctrine protects owners/GCs from double payment and is widely adopted (e.g., California — *Post Bros. Constr. Co. v. Yoder* (1977) 20 Cal.3d 1; Arizona; Washington). The co-payee protects itself by refusing to endorse or by obtaining a written allocation agreement. **No controlling NC appellate decision confirming NC's specific adoption of the joint-check rule was located in this research** — the book should present it as a widely-adopted doctrine and direct confirmation with NC counsel before relying on it as settled NC law.

### PART C — Pay-Application & Certification Machinery

**AIA G702/G703:** G702 (Application and Certificate for Payment) is the one-page cover certificate (nine-line roll-up: original contract sum → net change by COs → contract sum to date → completed & stored to date → retainage → earned less retainage → less previous certificates → current payment due → balance to finish). G703 (Continuation Sheet) is the line-item schedule of values feeding Line 4 of G702. The architect/certifier may certify an amount **different** from that applied for (per AIA A201 §§ 9.5 and 9.6), initialing all changed figures on G702/G703 and attaching an explanation. Under A201, applications are submitted at least 10 days before each progress-payment date.

**A201 §9.5.1 withholding grounds:** The architect may withhold or nullify a Certificate for Payment, in whole or in part, to protect the owner because of: defective work not remedied; third-party claims filed or reasonable evidence they will be filed; failure of the contractor to make payment to subcontractors/suppliers for labor/materials; reasonable evidence the work cannot be completed for the unpaid balance of the contract sum; damage to the owner or another contractor; reasonable evidence the work will not be completed within the contract time (and resulting liquidated damages); and persistent failure to carry out the work per the contract documents. **A201 (2017) § 9.4.1** gives the architect three options: certify in full; certify a portion and notify owner/contractor in writing of the reasons for withholding the remainder; or withhold the entire application with written reasons. **§ 9.5.3** lets the owner issue joint checks where certification is withheld for the contractor's failure to pay subs, and authorizes the owner to require written evidence of proper payment to subs. **§ 9.3.1 (2017)** lists releases and waivers of liens among the documents the owner/architect may require to accompany an application. **§ 9.6.8 (2017)** requires the contractor to defend and indemnify the owner against subcontractor liens after the owner has made "proper payment." Note: an architect's certification is treated as prima facie correct, with the burden on a challenger to show fraud or mistake (*Bolton Corp. v. T.A. Loving Co.*, 94 N.C. App. 392 (1989)).

**Partial-certification mechanics:** Certify by line item at verified percentages of actual installed value rather than applying a blanket lump-sum haircut; document withheld amounts by reason in separate, labeled buckets; treat **retainage** (a contractual percentage held from otherwise-earned amounts) and an **uncertified hold** (amounts not certified for cause under §9.5.1) as two distinct mechanisms with different release triggers; and always issue the written notice of reasons (A201 §9.4.1) to avoid procedurally defective withholding that triggers prompt-pay interest.

**NC prompt payment (private) — Chapter 22C (§§ 22C-1 to 22C-6):** Applies to private commercial construction but excludes residential projects of 12 or fewer units. **Owner-to-GC timing is NOT regulated** (governed by the contract). All down-chain payments (GC-to-sub, sub-to-sub, to suppliers) are due within **7 days** of the higher-tier party's receipt of payment; wrongfully withheld/late amounts accrue interest at **1% per month** beginning on the 8th day. **§ 22C-4** permits withholding for: unsatisfactory job progress; defective construction not remedied; disputed work; third-party claims filed or reasonable evidence a claim will be filed; failure of the subcontractor to make timely payments for labor/equipment/materials; damage to the contractor or another subcontractor; reasonable evidence the subcontract cannot be completed for the unpaid balance; or a reasonable amount for retainage not exceeding the initial percentage retained by the owner. **Pay-if-paid clauses are unenforceable in NC.** (Public-project prompt pay is separate, § 143-134.1: owner-to-prime within 45 days of acceptance/certification/occupancy, 1%/month interest thereafter; prime-to-sub within 7 days.)

### PART D — Abuse Patterns & Real Case Material

**Front-loading the schedule of values:** Assigning disproportionate value to early line items (mobilization, general conditions, sitework) to collect cash ahead of installed value. Sureties view modest front-loading as acceptable — an "accepted industry practice" to cover mobilization, insurance, and bond costs (Axcess Surety; IRMI) — but flag excessive loading as overbilling/contract liability. Industry guidance describes shifting ~3–7% of total contract value to early lines as typical (ProNovos), while loading ~50% of contract value into early-stage work "raises red flags for owners and auditors" (SmartBarrel) and can rise to a false-claim concern on public work (Levelset). **The mechanism that creates the owner's hole:** when the GC has been paid for more value than is physically in place and then walks off mid-job, the owner must complete the remaining work out of a contract balance that is now smaller than the cost-to-complete — the precise risk the "cash never ahead of verified work" rule guards against. Sources: Levelset, ProNovos, Axcess Surety, IRMI, Procore, Stackrows.

**Stored-materials billing abuse:** Off-site materials billing requires documentation before certification: bill of sale / title transfer to the owner, bonded or segregated storage with location identified, and insurance naming the owner. A201 § 9.3.2 cautions that if stored materials/equipment will not be paid until incorporated into the work, the provision (and the owner-contractor payment terms) must be modified, and ownership/risk-of-loss/insurance addressed. Owners typically require proof of title, location, and insurance as a condition of certifying stored-material lines.

**Retainage norms / law in NC:** There is **no statutory retainage cap for private projects** — retainage is governed by contract — but Chapter 22C provides that retainage withheld from a subcontractor on a private project may not exceed the percentage retained from the GC. For **public** projects (§ 143-134.1): retainage is capped at 5% of any periodic payment (and prohibited entirely on public contracts under $100,000); at 50% completion with satisfactory performance and surety consent, no further retainage is withheld (the owner may structure to hold 2.5% through completion); release within 60 days of substantial completion/occupancy; "early finishing trades" (e.g., structural steel, piling, caisson, demolition) get line-item release. Private commercial/condo work commonly uses 5–10% retainage by contract.

**Subcontractor nonpayment / slow-pay data:** Per **Rabbet's 2024 Construction Payments Report** (released October 1, 2024): "slow payments have cost the construction sector a staggering $280 billion in 2024," and "delayed payments have increased costs by 14% of total construction spending in 2024." The report finds "82% of contractors report payment delays of over 30 days — only 49% of contractors faced such delays just two years ago"; "95% of general contractors and 75% of subcontractors now float payments while awaiting developer disbursements"; and "100% of subcontractors factor in a general contractor's payment reputation when bidding." The average U.S. construction payment cycle is **90 days** — "double the 45-day threshold that financial analysts consider healthy for commercial enterprises." Corroborating figures: only 12% of construction businesses report always being paid on time (Levelset 2022); 97% of GCs increased bid prices in 2024 to absorb delays (Rabbet). (Note: Rabbet is a construction-finance software vendor; treat as industry data, not peer-reviewed research.)

**Funds-control / disbursement industry practice:** Title-company and funds-control agents administer escrow draws, perform title updates between draws to catch newly recorded mechanic's liens, sometimes conduct physical progress inspections, and disburse via single-payee or joint checks (often issuing single-payee checks directly to subs to ensure the money reaches them). **What they do NOT reliably do:** independently verify the *quality* of work in place (inspections confirm percentage complete, not workmanship), and they do not screen for *served* funds-lien notices unless specifically instructed. Lien-waiver-protection endorsements (e.g., ALTA 32/33 or "101" mechanic's-lien endorsements) require underwriter approval and typically a GC indemnity agreement; "with disbursing only" arrangements give no mechanic's-lien protection. Funds control is often framed as a cheaper alternative to a payment/performance bond (roughly half the cost) and a proactive (vs. reactive) risk tool. Sources: Partner ESI, Attorneys' Title Guaranty Fund, Land Title Guarantee Co., Procore, ZipBonds.

### PART E — Supporting Concepts

**CM-as-agent vs CM-at-risk vs GC; the means-and-methods liability line:** A construction-manager-as-agent (CMa / CM-agency) contracts only with the owner, owes the owner a fiduciary duty, holds no subcontracts, takes no schedule/budget risk, and provides no performance/payment bond — the owner holds the trade contracts (multi-prime). A CM-at-risk (or GC) holds the subcontracts, guarantees schedule/cost (often a GMP), and is "at risk" both to the owner and to subs for down-chain payment. **The liability line for an owner's rep:** an agent who begins paying subs directly or directing means and methods can convert itself into a de facto contractor and assume contractor-style liability. **"No privity":** an unpaid sub has no contract with the owner's rep; the rep should route such inquiries appropriately and avoid conduct (especially a series of direct payments) that can create a presumption of a contractual relationship and independent liability.

**Sworn statement / contractor's affidavit:** A "sworn construction statement" (or contractor's affidavit) lists every subcontractor and supplier and the dollar amount due each, giving the money-control function subcontract-level visibility **without** requiring production of the subcontracts themselves. Each draw, the sworn statement is reconciled against named, dollar-specific lien waivers to confirm down-chain payment. The AIA closeout/final-payment versions are **G706** (Contractor's Affidavit of Payment of Debts and Claims — certifies all payrolls, material bills, and indebtedness are paid except listed "exceptions," and may require a lien/indemnity bond for each exception; notarized), **G706A** (Contractor's Affidavit of Release of Liens), and **G707** (Consent of Surety to Final Payment / to reduction or release of retainage).

## Recommendations
- **Build each chapter from the verified citation set above.** Every scenario bullet can name a real section number, deadline, dollar threshold, named document, and (where available) a data point or case — enough for a chapter writer to draft from the research alone.
- **Lead the book with Scenarios 5 and 6.** The §44A-20(b) double-payment trap and the §44A-23(c) waiver nuance are the decision-critical, NC-specific hooks that justify the organizing rule; everything else is procedure built around them.
- **Flag the two recent changes prominently and date them correctly:** §22B-5 (eff. 3/1/2022) and the $40,000 lien-agent threshold (Session Law 2023-108, eff. 10/1/2023). **Correct any source or draft that attributes the threshold increase to Session Law 2024-30** — that is a DMV bill amending the unrelated §44A-4(b). Benchmark that would change this: a future session law amending §44A-11.1(a) again (watch the history line for a citation after "2023-108, s. 2(h)").
- **Treat the joint-check doctrine as persuasive, not settled NC law.** Have counsel confirm NC's application before any scenario states it as a binding NC rule; until then, present it as a widely-adopted doctrine with a confirm-with-counsel flag.
- **Include the standing disclaimer** in front matter and at the close of each scenario: this is practice/rehearsal material, not legal advice; confirm every decision with NC construction counsel licensed in the relevant county.

## Caveats
- **Joint-check doctrine:** well established in several states (CA, AZ, WA) but no controlling NC appellate decision confirming NC's specific adoption was located in this research; treat as persuasive authority.
- **Statutory inconsistency:** §44A-11.1(a) now reads "$40,000" while subsection (a1) (manufactured-home leasehold) still reads "$30,000" — an apparent legislative oversight that remains current statutory text.
- **Effective-date precision:** the $40,000 threshold's October 1, 2023 effective date is drawn from the UNC School of Government Legislative Reporting Service summary of HB 488 (a reputable secondary source); Session Law 2023-108 contained a mix of effective dates (Aug. 16, 2023; Oct. 1, 2023; Jan. 1, 2025), so for mission-critical precision the enrolled bill's effective-date section should be pulled verbatim. The threshold has, in any event, been operative since 2023.
- **Data sourcing:** the $280 billion slow-pay figure and related survey percentages are from Rabbet's 2024 Construction Payments Report, a construction-finance software vendor; cite as industry data, not peer-reviewed research. The "90,000+ liens in 12 months" NCS Credit figure could not be independently re-confirmed and should be verified before publication.
- **AIA editions:** section numbers cited are from the 2017 A201 (and the 1992 G702/G703, 1994 G706 series); confirm the edition that actually governs the project's owner-contractor agreement, since clause numbering shifts between editions.
- This dossier is research support for a practice book and is not legal advice.
