VOICE AND TONE:
Write narrative nonfiction driven by an argument. This is not a memoir and not
a textbook. The model is the popular-history tradition of John Barry (The Great
Influenza), Frank Snowden (Epidemics and Society), Steven Johnson (The Ghost
Map), and Laurie Garrett (The Coming Plague): a confident authorial voice that
tells true stories with novelistic detail and then steps back to say what they
mean. Lead in the third person and let the historical actors carry the scenes.
Use the first person only sparingly — to frame an argument, mark a judgment, or
signal uncertainty ("we still don't know"), never to narrate personal war
stories. The tone is intelligent, controlled, and quietly urgent. The writer
knows where the book is going and is unafraid to make claims, but earns them
with evidence rather than assertion.

THE THROUGHLINE:
This is the spine of the book and the most important instruction in this guide.
Every chapter must connect to one argument: there is a persistent gap between
what science knows about a disease and what a society actually does about it,
and that gap — not the pathogen — is usually where the catastrophe lives. The
science half keeps improving (we went from miasma theory to a sequenced genome
and a vaccine design in days). The society half does not reliably improve;
denial, delay, scapegoating, and broken trust recur across 2,500 years. The
payoff, which should build across the book and land hardest in the final part,
is the systems-thinker's punchline: as the science gets better, the gap matters
more, not less, because our tools now outrun our ability to deploy them. Return
deliberately to the two anchor experiments the structure is built around — Eyam's
voluntary quarantine in 1666, and Philadelphia versus St. Louis in 1918 — and
use them as fixed reference points the reader can navigate by.

TARGET AUDIENCE:
A smart, widely read generalist. Comfortable with systems, data, incentives, and
feedback loops, and quick to see structural patterns. Healthcare-literate enough
that you should not over-explain basic biology — define R-naught, herd immunity
threshold, and zoonotic spillover the first time, then trust the reader to keep
up. Market-research-minded, so the material on behavior, trust, information flow,
and why people believe what they believe will land and deserves real weight.
Write up to this reader, not down. Do not address the reader's profession
directly or make the book "about" any one person; the audience profile shapes
what you can assume, not what you mention.

READING LEVEL:
Educated general reader — a notch above the typical pop-nonfiction target.
Sophisticated sentences and subordinate clauses are fine in narrative passages;
tighten to short, declarative sentences when stating the argument or delivering a
number that matters. Define every technical term on first use, then use it
freely. Avoid both academic hedging and breathless oversimplification.

ACCURACY AND EPISTEMIC HUMILITY:
Treat this as a hard requirement, not a stylistic preference. The history of
epidemics is littered with confident wrong numbers, and this book's credibility
depends on getting the uncertainty right. Where the evidence is contested, say
so in the text rather than picking the most dramatic figure. Specifically:
- The 1918 influenza death toll is conventionally cited at 50–100 million but has
  been revised downward in some analyses toward ~17 million; give the range and
  note it is an estimate.
- COVID-19 has roughly 7 million confirmed deaths but excess-mortality estimates
  run 15–28 million; distinguish "confirmed" from "excess" every time.
- The pathogen behind the Plague of Athens is still disputed; do not name a
  culprit as settled.
- The origin of SARS-CoV-2 (zoonotic spillover vs. lab-related) has no
  scientific consensus; present both seriously and decline to adjudicate.
- The Fort Pitt smallpox-blanket episode is documented as intent but its actual
  epidemiological contribution is contested; keep that distinction.
- The "Disinformation Dozen" 65% figure is disputed by Meta; attribute it, don't
  assert it.
Never fabricate quotes, dates, or statistics. When a fact is uncertain, the
honest sentence is more persuasive than the tidy one.

ANECDOTES AND CASES:
Every chapter is anchored in real, named, dated cases — never generic
illustration. Use the specific people the history gives us: Thucydides, John
Snow, Ignaz Semmelweis, Mary Mallon, Ali Maow Maalin and the smallpox endgame,
Carlo Urbani and SARS, Katalin Karikó and mRNA. Detail is the engine of this
genre: the pump handle on Broad Street, the cordon stones at Eyam, the open-air
ward in St. Louis. If any scene is a composite or a reconstruction, label it as
such. Prefer the telling specific over the summarizing generality.

METAPHORS:
A set of recurring frames is available and should be used deliberately, not
decoratively: the epidemic as war, as fire, as flood, as mirror, as a stress
test of trust, as an accelerator of existing trends, and the Swiss-cheese model
of layered defense. Rotate them; do not staple the same metaphor to every
chapter. Engineering and systems analogies will resonate with this reader but
should be used with a light hand, not forced onto every page. One caution worth
making explicit in the text where relevant: Susan Sontag's critique of military
metaphors for illness — they can stigmatize the sick and distort policy — so use
the war frame knowingly. Avoid clichés ("at the end of the day," "the new
normal," "unprecedented").

SENSITIVE MATERIAL:
This book handles mass death, race, sexuality, religion, grief, and very recent
politically charged events. Treat all of it with dignity and seriousness. The
Strasbourg massacre, Tuskegee, the early AIDS years, and the racial dimensions
of the 1900 San Francisco plague require care and precision, not euphemism and
not sensationalism. For contested contemporary matters — COVID origins, the
2025–26 institutional retrenchment, vaccine politics, the WHO withdrawal —
present the competing positions fairly and let the throughline (trust, the gap)
do the analytic work; do not editorialize or score partisan points. The reader
should never be able to guess the author's voting record from the book.

NUMBERS AND CITATIONS:
Use specific figures, and give ranges and methodology where the brief provides
them (R-naught values, case-fatality vs. infection-fatality rates, death tolls,
budgets, vaccine-development timelines). A precise number with an honest
qualifier beats a round number stated flatly. Distinguish confirmed counts from
modeled estimates consistently.

DEFAULT WORD COUNT:
1,500 words per chapter unless otherwise specified. Several synthesis and
master-case chapters are intentionally longer (up to ~1,800); honor the
per-chapter target in the TOC where one is given.

HUMOR:
A light, dry touch is welcome — the absurdities of bureaucracy, the human comedy
of denial, the official who insists everything is fine as the wards fill. Keep it
wry and infrequent given the subject matter, and never at the expense of the
sick, the dead, or any group of people. The default register is serious; humor is
seasoning, not the dish.

STRUCTURE:
Follow the default chapter structure unless a chapter's content calls for
something different. Body sections use descriptive, content-specific subheadings
(not generic ones like "Section 1"). Open chapters with varied devices — a scene,
a single arresting fact, a question, an analogy — and avoid using the same
opening move in consecutive chapters. Close each chapter by tightening back to
the throughline and handing momentum to the next.
