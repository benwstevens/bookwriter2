# The CivicDev Pursuit Playbook: Table of Contents

*A book about how a private real estate developer wins municipal capital projects in North Carolina, South Carolina, and Virginia.*

## Preface — Why this book exists

**Why it matters.** A private developer pivoting into municipal owner's-rep work is entering a market with the same building-blocks (sites, drawings, contractors, dollars) but an entirely different operating system: different buyers, different money, different law, and a different sales cycle measured in years not weeks. This preface frames the gap between what a $2B-experience private developer already knows and the ~30% of the job that is genuinely new — and dangerous if learned on a live pursuit.

- The "small jurisdictions don't have a capital projects team" thesis and why it is durable in NC/SC/VA.
- What this book deliberately skips (private-side deal-making, financing, GC management) and why.
- How to read the book as a pursuit operator vs. a project executor.
- The 12–36 month B2G sales cycle and why patience is a capability.

# PART I — The Municipal Buyer

## Chapter 1. Why municipal buyers behave nothing like private clients

**Why it matters.** The single largest cause of failed pivots from private development to public-sector advisory is misreading the buyer. Municipal clients are not slow or irrational; they are optimizing for a different objective function — political survival, audit defensibility, and equity — that a private developer must learn to recognize before writing a single proposal.

- The three things every public buyer is privately optimizing for: defensibility, fairness, and re-election/retention.
- Why "speed and certainty" pitches that work on private LPs fall flat with town councils.
- The audit trail as the actual product the buyer is buying.
- Risk-aversion asymmetry: a public manager loses more from one bad project than they gain from ten good ones.

## Chapter 2. Forms of local government in NC, SC, and VA

**Why it matters.** Who can sign, who can champion, and who can kill a project depends on the form of government. NC and VA are dominated by council-manager and county-administrator structures; SC has a wider mix including strong-mayor cities and three distinct county forms. Misidentifying the form leads to lobbying the wrong person.

- Council-manager (NC default for cities; common in VA; common in SC) and the manager's authority over procurement.
- County structures: NC's manager-led counties, SC's three forms (council, council-administrator, council-manager, council-supervisor), VA's board of supervisors with county administrator or county executive.
- Strong-mayor and mayor-council variants and the "manager-in-name-only" pattern.
- Independent constitutional officers in VA (sheriff, commonwealth's attorney, clerk, treasurer, commissioner of revenue) and why they hold capital leverage.
- Where authority for capital project initiation, A/E selection, and contract award actually sits in each form.

## Chapter 3. Special-purpose districts and authorities

**Why it matters.** Many of CivicDev's best targets — fire stations, schools, water/sewer plants — are not run by general-purpose governments at all. Independent fire districts, school boards, and authorities have their own boards, their own procurement rules, and their own funding stacks, and they are systematically under-served by big consultants.

- NC fire service districts and county fire commissions; SC special purpose fire districts (e.g., Indian Land, Greater Greenville); VA fire/EMS departments embedded in counties or cities.
- Elected school boards in NC (county-coterminous) vs. SC's mixed appointed/elected boards vs. VA's elected/appointed split with county-funded capital.
- Water/sewer authorities, regional airport authorities, library systems, and recreation districts.
- Why district boards often lack staff capacity — the CivicDev sweet spot.

## Chapter 4. The career-staff buyer roster

**Why it matters.** In a pursuit, the proposal is read first by career staff and only then by elected officials. Knowing each role's daily pain, professional credentials, and conference circuit converts cold outreach into warm conversation. Most lost pursuits were lost in the staff read, not the council vote.

- County administrator / city manager / town manager and their deputies (ACM, ACA).
- Finance director and budget officer — the gatekeepers of CIP and bond capacity.
- Public works director, facilities/operations chief, capital projects manager or director.
- Department heads as project sponsors: fire chief, police chief, sheriff, school superintendent and chief facilities officer, library director.
- Procurement officer, planning director, engineering director — and their veto power over scope, schedule, and shortlist.

## Chapter 5. The elected-body buyer and how it actually decides

**Why it matters.** A council or board is not a monolith; it is a vote-counting exercise with predictable fault lines. Reading an elected body's voting record before writing an SOQ tells you which commissioners must be addressed in the cover letter, which fault lines to avoid, and whether the project has the four votes it needs to break ground.

- How to read 24 months of meeting minutes and votes to map a board.
- Predictable fault lines: urban/rural, fiscal-conservative/growth, geographic equity, race and district lines.
- The "swing seat" pattern and how A/E and OR firms get caught in it.
- Mayor-vs-manager tension and how to avoid being weaponized in it.
- How to identify the project champion, the skeptic, and the silent killer on every board.

## Chapter 6. What each buyer role reads, attends, and worries about

**Why it matters.** The pursuit letter that lands is the one that signals you read what they read. Each role has its own publications, conferences, listservs, and credentials, and matching those signals at the top of a letter is the single highest-leverage editing pass on any cold outreach.

- Manager track: ICMA, NCCCMA, SCCCMA, VLGMA; ICMA *PM Magazine*; the MPA pipeline.
- Finance track: GFOA, NCGFOA, SCGFOA, VGFOA; the *GFOA Best Practices*; CPFO/CGFM credentials.
- Fire track: IAFC, NCAFC, SCSFA, VFCA; *Fire Engineering*, *FireRescue1*; CFO/CTO credentials.
- Schools track: NCSBA, SCSBA, VSBA; superintendents' associations (NCSSA, SCASA, VASS); CASBO/ASBO for facilities chiefs.
- Public works track: APWA chapters; AWWA for water; engineering board licensure cycles.

## Chapter 7. Leadership transitions as windows and risks

**Why it matters.** A new manager, new chief, or new superintendent rewrites the capital priorities of a jurisdiction within their first 90 days. These transitions are the single highest-conversion pursuit windows — and the single highest-risk moments for an incumbent advisor. Tracking transitions across 150 miles of Raleigh is a daily BD discipline.

- The 90-day window and why outside advisors get pulled in to "help me get my arms around this."
- Interim managers and acting chiefs as accelerated decision-makers.
- Retirement waves in NC/SC/VA fire and school facilities leadership (post-COVID demographics).
- How to track transitions: state association job boards, local trade press, retirement announcements, LinkedIn signals.
- The risk of being the prior manager's vendor when a new one arrives.

# PART II — How a Project Becomes a Project

## Chapter 8. The municipal capital project lifecycle, end to end

**Why it matters.** Every pursuit decision — when to call, what to offer, what fee structure to propose — depends on which phase the project is in. A private developer's mental model collapses needs assessment, financing, and design into one compressed process; the municipal lifecycle stretches them across 3–7 years with formal gates between each.

- The eleven canonical phases: need → programming → site → environmental/zoning → CIP entry → funding → A/E procurement → design (SD/DD/CD) → CMR/builder procurement → construction → commissioning/FF&E/occupancy.
- Where outside owner's reps can plausibly enter at each phase, and where they cannot.
- The "phase confusion" problem: jurisdictions that issue an RFQ before they have a program or funding.
- How to diagnose phase from public artifacts (CIP, agendas, RFQ language).

## Chapter 9. Needs assessments and space-needs studies

**Why it matters.** This is the highest-leverage entry point for a CivicDev-style firm because it is pre-architect, pre-CIP-locking, and pre-political-commitment. A needs assessment well done determines the program, the budget, and often the architect — and the firm that runs it usually wins downstream advisory.

- What goes into a defensible needs assessment for fire, EMS, schools, courthouses, public works.
- Demographic and call-volume data sources (state fire marshal data, NC DPI/SC DOE/VDOE enrollment projections, U.S. Census).
- Capacity-vs-condition-vs-compliance framing and how to write a finding.
- Pricing a needs assessment ($30K–$150K range) and using it as a wedge for program management.
- Why architects systematically under-invest in needs assessments and where the gap lives.

## Chapter 10. Capital Improvement Plans — the gateway to every project

**Why it matters.** No project that is not on the CIP gets built. Understanding how CIPs are assembled, ranked, and adopted in NC/SC/VA local government is the precondition for every BD conversation; "how do we get this onto your CIP?" is often the first useful sentence in a discovery call.

- What a CIP is in NC/SC/VA practice, the rolling 5- and 10-year forms, and statutory requirements (NC GS 159-8 budget statute, VA Code §15.2-2239 planning commission CIP review).
- The annual cycle: department request → CIP committee → manager's recommended budget → board adoption.
- Ranking criteria and scoring rubrics (life-safety, mandate compliance, ROI, equity, growth-driven).
- The CIP committee composition: finance, planning, manager, sometimes citizen members.
- How outside advisors plug in: pre-request shaping, ranking-rubric design, cost validation, external review letters.

## Chapter 11. The politics of CIP ranking

**Why it matters.** The CIP is where geography, equity, and re-election math get encoded into a five-year capital schedule. Reading the politics of a CIP is reading the politics of the jurisdiction; misreading it leads to pursuing projects that will never get funded regardless of how good the SOQ was.

- The "every commissioner gets one" pattern and how it distorts ranking.
- Geographic equity rules (formal and informal) in rural NC/SC/VA counties.
- The fire-station-rotation pattern and how it interacts with ISO ratings.
- Why school capital almost always jumps the queue (mandate, growth, political salience).
- How a manager protects ranked priorities from late-stage political swaps.

## Chapter 12. Program management vs. project management — the distinction that changes everything

**Why it matters.** This is the conceptual hinge of the entire CivicDev offering. Program management sits at the CIP level and is owner-side, multi-project, strategic, and financial; project management sits at the single-project level and is construction-phase, tactical, and operational. They are scoped differently, procured differently, priced differently, and require different teaming structures.

- Program management scope: portfolio prioritization, funding strategy, A/E and CMR selection support, owner standards, cross-project schedule and budget rollups, risk and reporting.
- Project management scope: single-project owner's-rep functions — RFI/submittal review, pay-app validation, change-order discipline, OAC chairing, schedule analysis.
- How procurement language differs (program RFQs are broader, longer-duration, often IDIQ; project RFQs are narrower and tied to a CIP line).
- Fee structures: program management 1–3% of program construction value; project owner's-rep 3–7% depending on size and scope.
- When to bid as program manager, when as project manager, and when as both — and the firm-capacity implications.

## Chapter 13. Municipal funding mechanics — bonds, pay-go, and reserves

**Why it matters.** A pursuit without funding readiness is a pursuit that ends in a shelved CIP line. A private developer accustomed to capital stacks needs to learn the public-sector equivalents — GO, revenue, lease-revenue, COPs/LOBs, pay-go, capital reserves — and how each is approved, sized, and timed in the three target states.

- General Obligation (GO) bonds and the referendum requirement; NC LGC approval before any GO issuance; SC council-resolution path; VA referendum requirements by jurisdiction class.
- Limited Obligation Bonds (NC LOBs, formerly COPs/installment financings under GS 160A-20), SC certificates of participation, VA lease-revenue and Public Facility Revenue Bonds (PFRBs).
- Revenue bonds for water/sewer and parking; pay-go capital and capital reserves.
- The NC Local Government Commission as a unique pre-approval gatekeeper — why every NC local debt issuance ultimately runs through Raleigh.
- Reading a CAFR/ACFR and budget document for funding capacity, debt-service ratios, and unassigned fund balance.

## Chapter 14. State-specific capital funding programs

**Why it matters.** Each of the three target states has signature funding programs that an owner's rep must be able to discuss in a first call. Recognizing the program a project is being funded under tells you the procurement constraints, the timeline, and often the architect already in the picture.

- North Carolina: Needs-Based Public School Capital Fund (NBPSCF), State Capital and Infrastructure Fund (SCIF), Public School Building Capital Fund (Lottery), the quarter-cent local sales tax option (Article 46).
- South Carolina: Capital Project Sales Tax (CPST), Local Option Sales Tax (LOST), 1% education sales tax referenda, ACQ (Anticipation of Capital Qualifications) financing for districts.
- Virginia: VPSA pooled bonds, School Construction Assistance Program (SCAP), Literary Fund loans, the new School Construction Fund grants, regional 1% sales tax authority (recently expanded).
- Federal capital: USDA Community Facilities (loan + grant), FEMA BRIC and HMGP, EDA Public Works, Congressionally Directed Spending earmarks, DCIP/REPI for military-adjacent jurisdictions.
- ARPA tail and how SLFRF "obligated by 12/31/24, spent by 12/31/26" deadlines are still shaping pursuits.

## Chapter 15. Reading a budget document for funding readiness

**Why it matters.** Most pursuits are qualified or disqualified in 30 minutes with a budget document and a CIP. Learning to read these the way a private developer reads a pro forma is the single highest-ROI analytical skill in B2G capital pursuit.

- Where capital lives in a typical NC/SC/VA budget document (capital projects fund, debt service fund, special revenue funds).
- Funding-source columns and what "TBD" or "future bond" really mean.
- Debt service coverage, legal debt margin, and assigned/unassigned fund balance as readiness signals.
- The gap between "in the CIP" and "appropriated" — why projects sit for years.
- Red flags: chronic re-budgeting, ARPA dependency, declining fund balance, growing pension/OPEB liabilities.

# PART III — Procurement Law and the RFQ/RFP Universe

## Chapter 16. Qualifications-based selection and the mini-Brooks Acts

**Why it matters.** All three states require QBS for design professionals — and increasingly for related advisory services — meaning price cannot be the primary selection factor. This single legal principle reshapes how proposals are written, how fees are negotiated, and when fee discussions are even legal to have.

- The federal Brooks Act lineage and the state mini-Brooks statutes.
- North Carolina: GS 143-64.31 — qualifications-only first, fee negotiation second; the exemption thresholds.
- South Carolina: §11-35-3220 of the Consolidated Procurement Code.
- Virginia: VPPA §2.2-4302.1 and §2.2-4301 definitions of "professional services."
- The gray zone: does owner's-rep / program management / construction management advisory fall under mini-Brooks? How each state answers it (and how to position to be inside the QBS umbrella).

## Chapter 17. North Carolina procurement — GS 143 and the local landscape

**Why it matters.** NC's procurement framework is one of the more restrictive in the southeast, and the LGC overlay makes it unique. A firm pursuing NC work must speak fluent GS 143 by the third meeting or it will not be taken seriously by a finance director.

- GS Chapter 143 Article 8 (formal bidding), Article 3D (mini-Brooks), Article 8 thresholds ($500K formal construction, $90K informal, $30K small purchase as of last update).
- HUB (Historically Underutilized Business) goals and verification under NC OSHUB.
- NC Interactive Purchasing System (IPS) and the rise of OpenGov/DemandStar at the local level.
- LGC pre-approval for debt-financed projects (GS 159) and what it means for procurement timing.
- E-Verify, Iran Divestment Act, anti-BDS certifications required on NC contracts.

## Chapter 18. South Carolina procurement — the Consolidated Procurement Code

**Why it matters.** SC's Consolidated Procurement Code (Title 11, Chapter 35) is centralized and prescriptive at the state level, but local governments operate under their own ordinances that mirror or modify it. Knowing where state law ends and local ordinance begins is critical to writing a compliant SOQ.

- Title 11 Chapter 35 architecture and the Materials Management Office.
- Local government adoption: which counties and cities adopt the state code by reference vs. their own.
- Indefinite delivery contracts, cooperative purchasing, and emergency procurement under SC law.
- The SC Procurement Review Panel and protest procedures.
- MWBE participation under SC's Small and Minority Business Contracting and Certification Act.

## Chapter 19. Virginia procurement — VPPA and eVA

**Why it matters.** Virginia's VPPA is the most commercial-feeling of the three states' procurement regimes, and eVA is a mandatory state portal that even local solicitations often cross-post to. A firm not registered on eVA is invisible to a meaningful share of VA pursuits.

- VPPA architecture (Code of Virginia Title 2.2 Chapter 43) and how localities adopt or supplement.
- eVA registration, fees, and the eVA-only solicitation pattern.
- Public-Private Education Facilities and Infrastructure Act (PPEA) and Public-Private Transportation Act (PPTA) — when they unlock alternative procurement.
- Cone of silence rules and ex parte contact restrictions during active solicitations.
- Virginia's SWaM (Small, Women-owned, Minority-owned) certification and DBE pass-through requirements.

## Chapter 20. RFQ vs. RFP vs. cooperative purchasing — picking your lane

**Why it matters.** The same firm capability can be procured three different ways, with three different win economics. Recognizing which solicitation path a jurisdiction is on — and quietly steering toward the path that favors qualifications over price — is a craft skill.

- RFQ for QBS: qualifications, references, proposed team; fee negotiated post-selection.
- RFP for non-QBS advisory work: technical and price together, usually weighted.
- IFB/sealed bids: rarely applies to OR services but common adjacent for construction.
- Cooperative purchasing vehicles: Sourcewell, OMNIA Partners, NCEPP, GSA cooperative — when a piggyback contract avoids a full procurement.
- Sole source and emergency procurement: the narrow exceptions and how they get used (and abused).

## Chapter 21. Vendor registration, certifications, and compliance certs

**Why it matters.** Most lost pursuits at the small-firm level are lost on missing certs and registrations, not on substance. Building the certification stack once, correctly, is a one-time project that unlocks every solicitation in the three-state footprint.

- State portals: NC IPS/eVP, SC Vendor Self Service, VA eVA — registration mechanics and renewal cycles.
- Local government portals: OpenGov Procurement, DemandStar, BidNet, Periscope/mdf — and the duplication problem.
- HUB/SWaM/DBE/MBE/WBE certifications — when to pursue each, when to partner instead.
- Compliance certs that recur: E-Verify, Iran/Israel/anti-BDS divestment, drug-free workplace, conflict of interest.
- Insurance and bonding pre-qualification: COIs, professional liability, fidelity, performance/payment bonds (rare for OR but cited).

## Chapter 22. Evaluation criteria, weighting, shortlists, and debriefs

**Why it matters.** Behind every RFQ is an evaluation matrix — sometimes published, sometimes inferable. Reverse-engineering the matrix before responding is the difference between a generic SOQ and a winning SOQ. Post-loss debriefs are the most valuable free intelligence in the business.

- Typical OR/PM evaluation criteria: relevant experience, key personnel, approach, references, local presence, MBE participation.
- Common weight distributions in NC/SC/VA and how to read them.
- Shortlisting and oral interviews — when the panel meets, how long, who attends.
- Best-and-final offers (BAFOs) where allowed and where prohibited.
- Debrief practice: what to ask, what to write down, how to convert a loss into the next win.

# PART IV — Vertical Knowledge

## Chapter 23. Fire stations and the fire-service buyer

**Why it matters.** Fire stations are CivicDev's anchor vertical because of the partner's credibility — a former Raleigh Fire Chief with seven stations built is the single most valuable asset in the firm, and it must be deployed deliberately. Understanding how fire chiefs think, what they fear, and what makes them pick up the phone is the conversion engine of the firm.

- Career arc of a fire chief in NC/SC/VA: company officer → battalion → assistant chief → chief; lateral moves and how they map.
- Fire chief professional credentials: CFO (Chief Fire Officer), EFO (Executive Fire Officer), CTO; what each signals.
- Daily pain points: aging stations, apparatus lead times, staffing, ISO ratings, response times, decon/cancer compliance.
- Why the former-chief partner is decisive credibility — and how to deploy him without making him the only face.
- The "I've already talked to your peer in [adjacent jurisdiction]" opener.

## Chapter 24. Fire-service standards, accreditation, and metrics

**Why it matters.** A pursuit conversation about a fire station is incoherent without fluency in PPC, NFPA 1710/1720, and CFAI accreditation. These standards drive station siting, staffing, and program — and chiefs use them as the rationale to defend capital requests to councils.

- ISO Public Protection Classification (PPC) — how Class 1–10 ratings drive insurance and political pressure for new stations.
- CPSE/CFAI accreditation and the Standards of Cover document.
- NFPA 1710 (career) and 1720 (volunteer) response-time and staffing standards.
- Volunteer vs. combination vs. career departments — how the model changes the project.
- Cancer-prevention design standards (hot/warm/cold zones, decon, diesel exhaust capture) as a current capital driver.

## Chapter 25. Apparatus, prototypes, and station program elements

**Why it matters.** The reader's partner has built seven stations; CivicDev should be able to walk into any chief's office and discuss bay depth, apparatus lead time, and prototype economics in concrete terms. This is the credibility that closes the pursuit.

- Apparatus lead times (currently 3–4 years for engines/ladders) and how that drives station scheduling.
- Standard program elements: apparatus bays (drive-through vs. back-in), day room, dorms (gender-neutral trends), kitchen, decon, turnout storage, EOC/conference, PT/training, generator and tower.
- Prototype/repeatable design programs: Chesterfield County VA, Stafford County VA, Raleigh prototype, Mecklenburg standardization, Henrico prototypes — what works and what doesn't.
- Training facilities: burn buildings (Class A vs. gas-fired), tower, search/rescue props, classroom; cost ranges.
- EMS-only stations and combined fire/EMS — programmatic differences.

## Chapter 26. K-12 schools — governance and the capital funding split

**Why it matters.** Schools are the largest single bucket of municipal capital in the three target states, and the governance is uniquely bifurcated: an elected board hires a superintendent who runs the operation, but the county or state often controls the capital purse. Misreading that split kills pursuits.

- North Carolina: county funds capital, district operates; the county-district MOU pattern; LGC review of LOBs.
- South Carolina: districts issue 8% debt without referendum; ACQ financing; 1% sales tax referenda for capital.
- Virginia: county funds capital but uses VPSA pooled bonds, SCAP grants, and Literary Fund loans; the new School Construction Fund.
- The chief facilities officer / chief operations officer as the actual buyer for capital advisory.
- Educational specifications ("ed specs") — why they matter and who writes them.

## Chapter 27. K-12 procurement, demographics, and the design-build trap

**Why it matters.** School capital has its own procurement quirks, including statutory design-build authority that is being marketed as a faster path but actually shifts more scope-definition burden onto the owner — the exact place an owner's rep adds the most value.

- Demographic studies (Cropper GIS, MGT, Western Demographics) and how districts use them.
- Capacity analysis, redistricting risk, and the politics of consolidation.
- Architect-of-record vs. design-build vs. CMR for schools — when each is allowed in NC/SC/VA.
- Why design-build *increases* the owner's scope-definition burden and creates a larger OR opportunity, not a smaller one.
- LEED/CHPS/Energy Star, prototype reuse (e.g., Wake County prototypes), and the modular/permanent modular question.

## Chapter 28. Public safety campuses, EOCs, and 911 centers

**Why it matters.** Combined public safety campuses are an emerging vertical that bundles fire, police, EMS, and emergency management into a single capital project. They are larger, more complex, and more politically charged than single-use stations — and they reward firms that can credibly speak across all four user groups.

- Co-location economics: shared training, shared apparatus, shared admin.
- 911 PSAP standards (NENA i3), redundancy, and the consolidation trend.
- EOC design (NIMS-compliant, hardened, dual-use) and federal funding pathways.
- Police/sheriff capital: evidence storage, sally ports, holding, armory — sheriff vs. chief authority differences.
- Why these campuses often start as fire pursuits and grow.

## Chapter 29. Courthouses and detention — orientation only

**Why it matters.** Courthouses and jails are technically demanding, politically sensitive, and dominated by specialty firms. CivicDev should pursue them selectively but must understand the landscape to avoid being out-positioned when an adjacent fire/EMS pursuit grows into a public-safety-plus-courthouse program.

- NC AOC, SC Court Administration, and VA OES involvement in local courthouses; state space standards.
- Jail design standards: ACA accreditation, DOJ guidance, NC Jail Standards (10A NCAC 14J), SC Jail and Prison Inspection Office, VA Board of Local and Regional Jails.
- The sheriff as a constitutionally independent buyer (especially in VA).
- Why specialty firms (DLR, Moseley, HOK Justice, Mosely Architects) dominate and the small-firm subcontracting opportunity.
- Regional jail authorities in VA as a distinct buyer class.

## Chapter 30. Town halls, public works, libraries, and parks — orientation

**Why it matters.** These verticals are smaller, less competitive, and well-suited to a small OR firm building a portfolio. They are also the projects most likely to be under-served by the big consultants and most likely to be won on relationship and responsiveness.

- Town halls and admin buildings: chambers, public-meeting AV, clerk and finance space, security.
- Public works/maintenance facilities: vehicle bays, salt/sand storage, fuel, fleet wash, mezzanines.
- Libraries: the post-COVID program shift, makerspaces, state library funding (NC LSTA, SC SCSL, VA LVA grants).
- Parks and recreation: rec centers, aquatics, athletic complexes; PARTF (NC), PARD (SC), VLCF (VA) grant funding.
- Water/wastewater plants — flagged as a different procurement universe (engineering-led, SRF-funded) and largely out of scope for CivicDev.

# PART V — The Pursuit Motion

## Chapter 31. Building the lead pipeline

**Why it matters.** Pursuit volume is the leading indicator of revenue 18 months out. A disciplined lead-generation system that scrapes CIPs, tracks bond passages, and monitors RFQ portals across the three-state footprint is the operational backbone of the firm.

- CIP scraping: which jurisdictions publish, where, and how often they update.
- Bond referendum tracking: NC LGC agendas, SC State Election Commission results, VA bond election calendars.
- RFQ alerts: state portals, OpenGov, DemandStar, BidNet, Periscope; aggregator economics.
- News and Google Alerts: "capital improvement," "fire station," "school bond," "needs assessment," "[county] courthouse."
- The pursuit pipeline rating system — weighted ranks, deep-research files, tier-1/2/3 classification.

## Chapter 32. Qualifying the lead

**Why it matters.** Most firms lose money chasing unqualified leads. A disciplined qualification rubric — applied within an hour of identifying a lead — separates pursuits worth a deep-research file from those worth only an alert.

- Phase: where in the lifecycle is the project, and is it the phase where an OR adds value?
- Funding readiness: is money identified, appropriated, or just CIP-listed?
- In-house capacity: does the jurisdiction have a capital projects manager already?
- Leadership stability: how long has the manager been there, is a transition imminent?
- Fit to firm: project type, size, geography (within 150 mi of Raleigh), and competitive landscape.

## Chapter 33. The state and regional association BD universe

**Why it matters.** The NC/SC/VA municipal association ecosystem is the most efficient BD platform in the business — every relevant buyer attends one or two of these conferences a year, and the sponsorship economics are favorable for a small firm willing to commit. The reader should be a known face at six events a year within 18 months.

- North Carolina: NCLM (League of Municipalities), NCACC (Association of County Commissioners), NCCCMA (City/County Management Association).
- South Carolina: MASC (Municipal Association of SC), SCAC (SC Association of Counties), SCCCMA.
- Virginia: VML (Municipal League), VACo (Association of Counties), VLGMA (Local Government Management Association).
- Cross-state and national: ICMA, GFOA, APWA, NACo — for senior-buyer relationships.
- Vertical-specific: NC/SC/VA fire chiefs' associations, school boards' associations, school superintendents' associations.

## Chapter 34. Conferences, sponsorships, speaking, and strategic-partner programs

**Why it matters.** A sponsorship without a relationship strategy is wasted money. Speaking and panel slots convert better than booths; strategic-partner programs (where they exist) buy year-round access. Knowing the economics of each is how a small firm out-punches its weight class.

- Sponsorship vs. exhibit vs. speaking — relative cost and conversion.
- Strategic partner / corporate partner programs (NCLM, VML, ICMA) and what they actually unlock.
- How to land a panel slot: co-present with a manager or chief, propose a case-study session, lead with a former-chief partner.
- Off-program social events (golf, receptions) and where deals actually move.
- COGs and PDCs as sub-conference circuits: NC's 16 COGs, SC's 10 COGs, VA's 21 PDCs.

## Chapter 35. The cold pursuit letter — the firm's most important BD artifact

**Why it matters.** For a small advisory firm with a slow B2G sales cycle, the cold pursuit letter is the highest-leverage written artifact of the entire business. A well-calibrated letter to a manager or chief can open a 12-month conversation; a generic one is deleted in fifteen seconds. This chapter is treated in extended depth.

- The five-paragraph anatomy: localization, recognition of their problem, our specific relevance, proof, ask.
- Tone calibration by recipient role: manager (deference and brevity), chief (peer-to-peer credibility), finance director (numerical precision), elected official (constituent framing).
- Customization research: 30 minutes per letter — last 6 council meetings, last CIP, last newsletter, LinkedIn check.
- Finding the right addressee: title-not-name, name-not-just-title, when to copy whom.
- Red-flag research: ongoing litigation, recent scandal, pending recall, active procurement (cone of silence).
- Templates by recipient role and project type, with annotation on what to change and why.

## Chapter 36. The first call and discovery

**Why it matters.** A first call that is treated like a private-developer pitch will lose every time. The discovery call is a listening exercise designed to surface the buyer's framing of the problem, the political map, and the unspoken decision criteria — and to earn a second meeting, not a contract.

- The 70/30 listen-talk ratio and the opening question architecture.
- The "tell me where you are in the CIP cycle" opener and what the answer reveals.
- Reading the org chart in real time — who else needs to be in the next conversation?
- The soft offer: a free desk-top review, a memo, a benchmarking conversation — never a proposal on call one.
- Note discipline and CRM hygiene immediately after the call.

## Chapter 37. Shaping the RFQ before it issues

**Why it matters.** The RFQs that small firms win are usually the ones that small firms helped scope. Pre-RFQ engagement is legal in all three states under specific conditions, and the firms that master it convert at multiples of the firms that respond cold.

## Chapter 38. The SOQ/proposal package

**Why it matters.** The SOQ is the artifact the evaluation panel actually scores. Every formatting choice, every key-personnel sheet, every project sheet, every fee structure is downstream of the evaluation matrix. A craft-level SOQ is the difference between shortlisted and discarded.

# PART VI — Pricing, Positioning, and the Firm

## Chapter 40. How municipalities expect to see fees

**Why it matters.** A fee proposal that does not match the format the procurement officer expects is a fee proposal that gets kicked back or scored low on responsiveness. Each state and each delivery method has conventions, and the fee section of an SOQ is where many firms quietly lose.

- Fee formats: percentage of construction value, lump sum by phase, hourly NTE, hybrid (lump-sum-pre-construction + percent-during-construction).
- When fee is requested in the SOQ vs. negotiated post-shortlist (mini-Brooks rules).
- Reimbursable expenses: what's typically allowed, what's typically capped, and the markup conventions.
- Multi-year fee structures and escalation clauses.
- The "compare apples to apples" problem and how to make your fee structure easy to score.

## Chapter 41. Owner's-rep fee benchmarks and structures

**Why it matters.** Pricing too high loses pursuits; pricing too low destroys the firm. Knowing the actual fee ranges for OR work in NC/SC/VA — by project type, size, and scope — is the difference between profitable growth and unprofitable busy-ness.

- Program management: 1–3% of total program construction value, often capped or hourly NTE.
- CM advisory / construction-phase OR: 3–5% of construction value.
- Full owner's-rep (programming through occupancy) on small projects: 4–7% of construction value.
- Needs assessments and pre-CIP advisory: $30K–$150K lump sum.
- How to benchmark against published peers: peer ORs (Brailsford & Dunlavey, Heery, Cumming, Faithful+Gould, Vanir), regional firms, and recent NC/SC/VA award notices.

## Chapter 42. Positioning CivicDev as "your capital projects team"

**Why it matters.** The firm's core value proposition — "small jurisdictions don't have a capital projects team; we add that capacity" — is stronger and more defensible than any consultant-pitch alternative. Every BD artifact, every conversation, and every proposal should reinforce that positioning.

- The capacity frame vs. the consultant frame — language differences and why they matter.
- "We are your capital projects team" as the through-line in the cover letter, the SOQ, and the interview.
- How to embody capacity without overstating embeddedness (and the legal risk of being treated as an employee).
- Counter-positioning against the big OR firms (we are local, embedded, dedicated; they are regional, transactional).
- Counter-positioning against the architect (we are owner-side; the architect is contractor-side).

## Chapter 43. The credibility hierarchy small jurisdictions trust

**Why it matters.** Buyers in small NC/SC/VA jurisdictions follow a specific credibility hierarchy that does not match the credentials a private developer accumulated. Knowing which signals work and which don't is the difference between being heard and being skipped.

- Tier 1: former public-sector executive (former chief, manager, superintendent) on the team — the highest-trust signal.
- Tier 2: peer firm that served three similar-sized similar-region jurisdictions, with named references.
- Tier 3: generalist consultant with case studies but no public-sector alumni.
- The "are you a developer or a consultant?" question and how to answer it without losing the room.
- Why $2B in private construction matters less than seven fire stations built — and how to weight the pitch accordingly.

## Chapter 44. Proof points, case studies, and first-win economics

**Why it matters.** A firm with one public-sector case study converts at multiples of a firm with zero. The first three wins are not about revenue — they are about case-study acquisition cost. Pricing and scope on those first three should reflect that.

- Case study architecture: the public-sector one-pager (problem, role, outcome, references, dollar value, dates).
- Reference cultivation: who to ask, when, how often to reach back.
- Named project alumni — the long-tail value of staying in touch with managers who move.
- First-win pricing: what to discount, what never to discount (rate, scope clarity), and how to recover on year two.
- Published thought leadership as a substitute for case studies in the early months.

## Chapter 45. Staffing a pursuit-phase firm

**Why it matters.** The org chart of a pursuit-phase advisory firm is different from a project-execution firm. CivicDev needs originator capacity, SME credibility, and pursuit-research throughput long before it needs full-time PMs. Staffing in the wrong order burns runway.

- Principal/originator: the relationship operator and primary author.

- Fire-service SME: the partner; deployment economics and protection of bandwidth.
- Schedule/cost analyst: the proposal and pursuit-support role.

**Why it matters.** The reader is already using Claude and deep-research workflows; systematizing these into a repeatable pursuit operation is the firm's quiet competitive advantage. A two-person firm that runs disciplined LLM-assisted pursuit research can outwork a ten-person firm that doesn't.

- The deep-research file template: jurisdiction profile, leadership map, CIP summary, project history, political map, red flags.

# PART VII — Risk, Ethics, Insurance, Partnering

## Chapter 48. Insurance and liability for owner's-rep firms

**Why it matters.** Owner's-rep firms occupy an unusual liability position — they advise but do not design or build, and their insurance must reflect that. Getting the coverage stack right is both a procurement requirement and a survival requirement.

- Professional liability (E&O) for OR firms — typical limits ($1M/$2M to $5M/$10M), carriers, exclusions.
- General liability, auto, workers' comp, and umbrella — the standard public-contract package.
- Cyber liability — increasingly required on contracts touching public records.
- The "we are not the architect of record" line and why it must appear in every contract.
- Indemnification and limitation-of-liability clauses — what's negotiable in NC/SC/VA municipal contracts.

## Chapter 49. Public-contract terms unique to municipal clients

**Why it matters.** Municipal contracts contain clauses that don't exist in private contracts and that can quietly destroy firm economics if not negotiated. Recognizing them on first read of a contract draft is a basic competence.

- Termination for convenience and how to structure fees to survive it.
- Fund-out / non-appropriation clauses — the contract dies if the council doesn't fund next year.
- Pay-when-paid and the slow-payment reality of municipal AP cycles.
- Sovereign immunity and why the OR has limited remedies against the public client.
- Lien rights — you generally don't have them as an advisor, and the contract should not pretend you do.
- Dispute escalation: contract administrator → manager → mediation → litigation; arbitration rarely available in public contracts.

## Chapter 50. Ethics, gifts, and political contributions in NC, SC, and VA

**Why it matters.** A single gift-rule violation or improperly disclosed political contribution can disqualify a firm permanently in a small market. Each state has different rules and different enforcement temperatures, and a private developer's habits around lunches, donations, and event tickets do not transfer.

- North Carolina: State Ethics Act, Local Government Ethics Act (GS 160A-86), gift limits, lobbyist registration thresholds.
- South Carolina: State Ethics Act, lobbyist principal registration, gift bans for officials.
- Virginia: State and Local Government Conflict of Interests Act, the $100 gift threshold, statement of economic interests.
- Political contributions: pay-to-play rules, school-board and council-candidate contribution limits, disclosure traps.
- The "do not accept the gift" default and how to manage social events without violations.

## Chapter 51. Transparency, FOIA, and open meetings as BD tools

**Why it matters.** Public-records and open-meetings laws are usually framed as compliance burdens, but for a pursuit firm they are an intelligence platform. Mastering them turns every NC/SC/VA jurisdiction into a transparent prospect.

- North Carolina Public Records Act (GS 132) and Open Meetings Law (GS 143-318); NC's relatively permissive standard.
- South Carolina FOIA (§30-4-10 et seq.) and the 10-day response window.
- Virginia FOIA (§2.2-3700) and its narrower exemption set.
- How to attend, listen at, and use council/board meetings; agendas, packets, and consent items as research gold.
- The records request as a relationship-builder (or destroyer) — when to file, when not to.

## Chapter 52. Conflict-of-interest patterns and scandal pathology

**Why it matters.** The history of NC/SC/VA municipal capital projects is dotted with conflict-of-interest scandals that cost firms their licenses, reputations, and freedom. Recognizing the patterns protects the firm; understanding them protects the firm's positioning when scandals near a target jurisdiction.

- The Greensboro/Samet/Matheny pattern and what it teaches about contractor-staff relationships.
- The "former employee, current vendor" pattern and the cooling-off rules.
- The "consultant who wrote the RFQ also bid on it" pattern.
- Disclosure obligations when a principal previously worked for a contractor or A/E in the market.
- The reputational halo: how an adjacent scandal taints adjacent firms and how to insulate.

## Chapter 53. Teaming — prime vs. sub, with whom, and when

**Why it matters.** A small OR firm wins more work as a sub in year one and as a prime in year three. Choosing teaming partners is a multi-year strategic decision, not a per-pursuit tactical one, and the wrong prime can lock the firm out of a relationship for years.

- When to be the prime: small jurisdictions, OR-led pursuits, where CivicDev's positioning is the brand.
- When to be the sub: large/complex pursuits, A/E-led teams, CMR advisory roles.
- Choosing primes: A/E firms vs. national OR firms vs. CMRs — the relationship-rights implications of each.
- Teaming agreement essentials: scope, fee split, exclusivity, follow-on work, client-relationship rights.
- The "do not let the prime steal the client" clause and how to enforce it in practice.

## Chapter 54. MBE/WBE/HUB partnership economics

**Why it matters.** Diverse-business participation is required on most public contracts, and how a firm engages MBE/WBE/HUB partners says as much about the firm as its case studies. Token partnerships are detected and penalized; real partnerships unlock a different tier of relationships.

- NC HUB, SC SMBCC, VA SWaM/DBE — certification requirements and reciprocity.
- Real participation vs. paper participation — what evaluation panels see.
- Mentor-protégé and joint-venture structures.
- Long-term diverse-business partner cultivation as a multi-year BD asset.
- The "second-tier" pattern: when CivicDev itself can be the sub on someone else's MBE/WBE-led pursuit.

## Chapter 55. Owner-consultant contracts — AIA C-series and EJCDC

**Why it matters.** When a jurisdiction uses a published industry contract template, the OR needs to know which template, which clauses to amend, and which to leave alone. Walking into a negotiation without that fluency means accepting bad terms or losing the contract.

- AIA C132 (Owner-CM-as-Adviser) and C133 (Owner-CM-as-Constructor) — when each applies and where the OR sits.
- AIA C172 (Owner-Program Manager) — the program-management agreement.
- EJCDC E-series for engineer-led owner-side advisory.
- Owner-generated contracts: how NC/SC/VA local governments often draft their own and what to redline.
- The flow-down problem: how owner-A/E and owner-CMR terms flow down to the OR scope.

# PART VIII — From Win to Execution (Just Enough to Be Credible)

## Chapter 56. Week one — what an owner's rep actually does

**Why it matters.** This is the question every interview panel asks: "What would you actually do for us starting Monday?" A principal who answers it specifically and concretely wins; a principal who answers it generically loses. This chapter is the answer in long form.

- Day 1–10: stakeholder map, document audit, baseline schedule, baseline budget, risk register.
- Day 11–30: charter the project, establish OAC cadence, establish reporting templates, kick off A/E or pre-construction.
- The first board update — what to say, what not to say, how to set expectations.
- The "what we found" memo and how it positions the OR for years two and three.
- How to brief the manager privately before the first public update.

## Chapter 57. Programming and scope-budget reconciliation

**Why it matters.** Most municipal projects fail at the seam between programming and budget — the program is sized for what users want, the budget is sized for what was guessed at the CIP stage, and the gap surfaces only at SD. The OR's job is to surface that gap before the architect does.

- Program validation: square footage by space type vs. peer-jurisdiction benchmarks.
- Cost benchmarking: $/SF for fire stations, schools, town halls in NC/SC/VA in current dollars.
- The reconciliation memo: what the program costs vs. what the budget allows vs. what the gap closes.
- VE strategies that don't gut the program: deferred FF&E, phased construction, shell-and-fit-out, alternates.
- Communicating bad budget news to the manager without losing the relationship.

## Chapter 58. A/E selection and contract negotiation, owner-side

**Why it matters.** Selecting and negotiating the architect is often the highest-leverage thing an OR does for a small jurisdiction, because it sets the trajectory of the whole project. Knowing how to run a QBS A/E procurement and negotiate fees post-shortlist is a core OR skill.

- Running the A/E RFQ: scope, evaluation criteria, shortlist, interviews, ranking.
- Fee negotiation under mini-Brooks: scope-then-fee, walking-away rights, secondary candidate.
- Owner standards: what the OR asks the A/E to comply with that the jurisdiction couldn't have specified itself.
- The A/E contract: AIA B-series amendments commonly required by public clients.
- Managing the A/E relationship without becoming an A/E.

## Chapter 59. Delivery method selection — DBB, CMR, and design-build

**Why it matters.** The delivery method is often chosen before the OR is on board — and often chosen badly. Reframing the choice for a manager or board, and explaining the owner-side resource implications of each, is a core advisory function.

- Design-bid-build: cheapest in theory, slowest in practice, hardest on the owner during bids.
- CM-at-Risk (CMAR/CMR): the dominant NC/SC/VA method for mid-size public projects; GMP economics.
- Design-build: faster but higher owner-side scope-definition burden; statutory authority in each state.
- Progressive design-build and IPD — niche but emerging.
- The "match delivery method to owner capacity" principle and where the OR fits in.

## Chapter 60. GMP negotiation and value engineering

**Why it matters.** GMP negotiation is where projects make or lose 5–10% of total cost. Walking the manager and finance director through a GMP — line by line, contingency, fee, GCs/GRs, allowances, alternates — is exactly the moment the OR earns its fee for the year.

- GMP anatomy: cost of work, GC/GR, fee, contingency (owner vs. CMR), allowances, alternates.
- Open-book economics and the audit rights every public GMP should preserve.
- VE before GMP vs. VE after GMP — and why post-GMP VE is usually a sign of pre-GMP failure.
- Subcontractor bidding under CMR: the three-bid rule, the self-perform exception, the public-bid-list-disclosure requirement (varies by state).
- Contingency burndown and how to report it to the board.

## Chapter 61. OAC meeting cadence and project controls

**Why it matters.** The OAC (owner-architect-contractor) meeting is the project's nervous system, and the OR usually chairs it. Running a tight OAC, with disciplined RFI/submittal/change-order management, is the most visible thing the OR does week-to-week.

- OAC cadence (weekly construction, biweekly pre-construction) and standing-agenda discipline.
- RFI log management: response-time metrics, cost-impact flags, escalation rules.
- Submittal log: critical-path submittals, long-lead procurement (apparatus, switchgear), approval cycles.
- Change-order discipline: the change-order cover sheet, the owner-decision deadline, contingency draw.
- Pay application review: schedule-of-values discipline, lien waivers, retainage release.

## Chapter 62. Permitting, commissioning, FF&E, and closeout

**Why it matters.** The last 10% of the project is where reputations are made. A clean closeout — punch list, commissioning, FF&E coordination, occupancy — converts a satisfied client into a referenceable one. A messy closeout undoes the goodwill of the prior 24 months.

- DRB/permitting cadence in NC/SC/VA jurisdictions and how to manage agency review.
- Commissioning: enhanced (LEED) vs. fundamental, the Cx agent's relationship to the OR, fire-station-specific Cx (apparatus exhaust, alerting, generator).
- FF&E procurement: separate procurement, often state cooperative contracts; coordination with construction completion.
- Punch list, substantial completion, final completion, retainage release.
- The 11-month warranty walk and how the OR uses it as the bridge to the next pursuit.

## Chapter 63. Turning a finished project into the next pursuit

**Why it matters.** The most valuable asset a finished project produces is not the building — it is the case study, the references, and the alumni relationships with the manager, chief, and board. Systematizing the harvest of these assets is how a one-project firm becomes a fifteen-project firm.

- The closeout memo that becomes the case study.
- Reference cultivation in the first 90 days post-occupancy and again at the 12-month mark.
- Alumni tracking: where did the manager, chief, superintendent go next?
- The ribbon-cutting and what to do with it (and what not to).
- Publishing: trade press, association magazines, conference panels — turning the project into thought leadership.
