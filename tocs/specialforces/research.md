# When special operations go wrong: 15 consequential US SOF failures

**The history of US special operations forces is defined as much by catastrophic failure as by celebrated triumph.** From the empty POW camp at Son Tay in 1970 to the ambush at Tongo Tongo in 2017, each disaster exposed systemic weaknesses — intelligence breakdowns, inter-service rivalry, mission creep, inadequate oversight — that cost American lives and reshaped how the nation fights its shadow wars. These failures drove the creation of every major SOF institution that exists today: JSOC, the 160th SOAR, USSOCOM itself. What follows are 15 of the most consequential SOF mishaps in American military history, structured as detailed case files for reference.

---

## 1. The Son Tay Raid found an empty prison (November 21, 1970)

**Units:** 56 Army Special Forces soldiers (6th and 7th SFGs), USAF Special Operations, Navy diversionary forces | **Location:** Son Tay, North Vietnam, 23 miles west of Hanoi

**What happened:** Under BG LeRoy Manor and COL Arthur "Bull" Simons, Green Berets executed a near-flawless tactical raid on the Son Tay prisoner-of-war camp — crash-landing a helicopter in the compound courtyard, breaching walls, clearing buildings — all in 27 minutes. The Navy launched its largest night carrier operation of the war as a diversion. The operation was a masterclass in joint SOF execution with one devastating problem: **the 65 POWs had been moved four months earlier**, on July 14, 1970, due to flooding that contaminated the camp's wells.

**What went wrong:** The root cause was extreme compartmentalization. The Joint Contingency Task Group was deliberately isolated from normal intelligence channels to maintain operational security, which prevented receipt of timely updates on the camp's status. Satellite imagery was obscured by monsoon rains. Intelligence confirming the prisoners had been moved arrived only two days before the raid — too late to redirect. Additionally, COL Simons's element landed at a wrong compound 400 meters south with a nearly identical layout, where they engaged and killed an estimated 100–200 foreign military personnel (possibly Chinese or Soviet advisors) before recognizing the error.

**Casualties:** Two minor US injuries. No POWs rescued. Two aircraft lost (one deliberately crash-landed). The raid was publicly condemned as an intelligence embarrassment, though POWs later reported significantly improved treatment afterward.

**Aftermath:** The Son Tay model — joint planning by operators, extensive rehearsals (170+ at Eglin AFB), purpose-built training mockups — became the template for future SOF missions. It was the first joint operation under direct JCS Chairman control. The intelligence failure drove a **major reorganization of the US intelligence community** and highlighted the lethal tension between OPSEC compartmentalization and real-time intelligence access — a tension that would recur in nearly every subsequent failure on this list.

**Key sources:** Benjamin Schemmer, *The Raid*; John Gargus, *The Son Tay Raid*; BG Manor's official after-action report

## 2. Desert One destroyed eight lives and one presidency (April 24–25, 1980)

**Units:** Delta Force (Squadrons A and B), USMC helicopter pilots, USAF Special Operations (MC-130s, EC-130s), Army Rangers, CIA Special Activities Division | **Location:** "Desert One," Great Salt Desert, Iran, ~200 miles southeast of Tehran

**What happened:** Operation Eagle Claw was an audaciously complex plan to rescue 53 American hostages from the US Embassy in Tehran. The two-night scheme required C-130s and eight RH-53D Sea Stallion helicopters to rendezvous at a remote desert strip, refuel, fly Delta Force to a mountain hideout near Tehran, then extract hostages via CIA vehicles, helicopters, and a Ranger-seized airfield — with C-141s flying everyone to Egypt. The plan never made it past Night One.

**What went wrong — a cascade of failures:** Of eight helicopters launched from USS Nimitz, only five arrived operational. Helicopter #6 turned back with a cracked rotor blade. #5 became disoriented in a *haboob* (dust storm) no one predicted and returned to the carrier. #2 suffered hydraulic failure from sand-clogged filters and was grounded at Desert One. The RH-53Ds were minesweeping helicopters, **not designed for long-range desert operations**, stored on Nimitz's hangar deck with minimal maintenance for months. The Navy had refused COL Charlie Beckwith's request for 10–12 helicopters, insisting only eight could be accommodated.

With five operational helicopters — one below the mission-abort threshold of six — President Carter ordered withdrawal. During repositioning, a helicopter pilot blinded by rotor-wash dust pushed forward into an EC-130 tanker. The resulting explosion and fireball killed **eight servicemen** — five Air Force crew and three Marines. Seven helicopters and one C-130 were abandoned along with all classified mission plans.

The deeper failures were structural. No unified SOF command existed. Delta trained in North Carolina, helicopter crews at Yuma, Air Force crews in Florida — **never fully rehearsing together as a combined force.** Marine helicopter pilots were told to fly below 200 feet to avoid radar; CIA reconnaissance had found no radar below 3,000 feet. No full-scale dress rehearsal was ever conducted.

**Casualties:** 8 killed (Maj. Richard Bakke, Maj. Harold Lewis Jr., TSgt. Joel Mayo, Capt. Lyn McIntosh, Capt. Charles McMillan, Sgt. John Harvey, Cpl. George Holmes Jr., SSgt. Dewey Johnson). Five additional personnel severely burned. All classified documents compromised.

**Consequences:** The hostages were scattered across Iran to prevent a second attempt and were not released until Reagan's inauguration — 444 days total. Carter later blamed the crisis for his election loss. The Holloway Commission identified **23 areas of concern** and gave the mission only a 60% chance of success even if everything had worked.

**Aftermath — the most consequential failure in SOF history:** Eagle Claw directly produced:
- **Joint Special Operations Command (JSOC)** at Fort Bragg
- **160th Special Operations Aviation Battalion ("Night Stalkers")**, established October 1981 to provide dedicated SOF helicopter capability
- The **Goldwater-Nichols Act (1986)** restructuring all armed forces for "jointness"
- **United States Special Operations Command (USSOCOM)**, activated April 1987

Every major SOF institution in existence today traces its origin to the wreckage at Desert One.

**Key sources:** Holloway Commission "Rescue Mission Report" (1980); Col. James Kyle, *The Guts to Try*; Charlie Beckwith, *Delta Force*; Mark Bowden, "The Desert One Debacle," *The Atlantic*

## 3. Grenada exposed SOF's most fundamental vulnerabilities (October 25, 1983)

**Units:** SEAL Teams 4 and 6, Delta Force, 1st/2nd Ranger Battalions, 160th SOAR (combat debut), USAF Combat Controllers | **Location:** Grenada

**What happened:** Operation Urgent Fury was ordered after the execution of PM Maurice Bishop, with the Iranian hostage crisis fresh in memory and 600 American medical students at risk. Planning was measured in days, not weeks. Intelligence was so poor that planners relied on **tourist maps**. Then H-hour was pushed from 0200 to 0500 by conventional commanders — forcing SOF to operate in broad daylight, the most fundamental violation of special operations doctrine.

**What went wrong — everything, simultaneously:**

*SEAL Team drownings (October 23):* Twelve personnel parachuted at night from a C-130 to conduct pre-assault reconnaissance of Point Salines airfield. **Four SEALs drowned** — overburdened with equipment, entangled in parachutes in rough seas. A second attempt the next night also failed. Rangers jumped blind into Point Salines with zero pre-assault intelligence.

*Richmond Hill Prison — Delta Force:* B Squadron's six Black Hawks arrived 5.5 hours late, in daylight. They had no accurate maps, no knowledge of defenses, and no building blueprints. Approaching the prison, they discovered the hillside was too steep to land and **two ZU-23-2 anti-aircraft guns** commanded the approach from Fort Frederick. Withering fire shot down one Black Hawk, killing **CPT Keith Lucas** — the 160th SOAR's first combat fatality — and damaged five others. Delta never entered the prison.

*Governor-General's mansion — SEAL Team 6:* Twenty-two SEALs secured Governor-General Scoon but were immediately besieged by Grenadian forces with BTR-60 armored personnel carriers. Their communications equipment was on a helicopter that had aborted, forcing them to **call for air support via international collect call using a soldier's personal credit card.** They were trapped for 24 hours until relieved by 250 Marines.

*Communications catastrophe:* Different services could not communicate with each other due to incompatible radio systems — the defining failure of the entire operation.

**Casualties:** SOF accounted for **13 of the 19 total US fatalities** — a staggering proportion given their small numbers. Four SEALs drowned, one 160th pilot killed, additional personnel killed in helicopter shootdowns and ground actions. One MH-60 Black Hawk destroyed, two Marine Cobras shot down, five Black Hawks damaged — all by a 45-man anti-aircraft battery.

**Aftermath:** MG Richard Scholtes, the JSOC commander, blamed SOF losses on ad hoc organization and misuse of SOF by conventional commanders. His closed-door congressional testimony was instrumental in passing the **Nunn-Cohen Amendment (1987)**, which established USSOCOM. Grenada demonstrated the critical need for dedicated SOF intelligence, compatible inter-service communications, and respect for SOF operational requirements by conventional commanders.

**Key sources:** Mark Adkin, *Urgent Fury*; Ronald Cole, *Operation Urgent Fury* (Joint History Office); Eric Haney, *Inside Delta Force*

## 4. Paitilla Airport was a "jobs program" that killed four SEALs (December 20, 1989)

**Units:** 48 SEALs from SEAL Team 4 (Platoons Golf, Bravo, Delta) | **Location:** Punta Paitilla Airport, Panama City, Panama

**What happened:** During Operation Just Cause, SEAL Team 4 was assigned to disable Noriega's personal Learjet to prevent his escape. The mission was originally assigned to Army units. **Navy commanders, unhappy that no naval units had a role, lobbied to give SEALs the Paitilla mission** — an airfield seizure task far better suited for Rangers.

**What went wrong:** The original plan called for destroying the aircraft from standoff distance. Orders were progressively changed to "disable with minimal damage" — shoot out tires, cut control wires — forcing SEALs to approach dangerously close. Three safer alternative plans were proposed by SEAL planners; all were rejected by Commodore John Sandoz. One SEAL officer refused to endorse the plan and was moved from operations to logistics.

As Golf Platoon stood to move into ambush positions against approaching PDF armored vehicles, PDF soldiers opened fire. **All but one SEAL in Squad One were hit** in the initial volley — two killed instantly, five wounded. In the ensuing firefight, two more SEALs died and four more were wounded. Chief Engineman Don McFaul heroically dragged wounded teammates to safety before being mortally wounded (Navy Cross, posthumous).

**Casualties:** 4 SEALs killed (Lt. j.g. John Connors, ENC Donald McFaul, BM1 Christopher Tilghman, TM2 Isaac Rodriguez III). 8–9 wounded — approximately **25% of the assault force**. The Learjet was eventually destroyed by an AT-4 rocket — the method that should have been used from the start.

**Aftermath:** Paitilla became one of the most controversial operations in SEAL history and a case study in the dangers of **inter-service competition for missions**. The operation demonstrated that a 48-man SEAL platoon was too lightly armed to hold an airfield, that mission creep during planning ("minimal damage") forced unnecessarily dangerous tactics, and that the aircraft could have been destroyed safely from standoff distance or by air strike. USS McFaul (DDG-74) was later named for CPO McFaul.

**Key sources:** Lawrence Yates, *The US Military Intervention in Panama* (US Army CMH); Kurt Muse, *Six Minutes to Freedom*; National Navy UDT-SEAL Museum archives

## 5. Mogadishu turned a 30-minute raid into a 15-hour battle (October 3–4, 1993)

**Units:** Task Force Ranger — Delta Force, B Company/3rd Battalion/75th Ranger Regiment, 1st Battalion/160th SOAR, DEVGRU operators, Air Force Combat Controllers and PJs | **Location:** Bakaara Market district, Mogadishu, Somalia

**What happened:** Operation Gothic Serpent aimed to capture two of Mohamed Farrah Aidid's lieutenants near the Olympic Hotel. The mission was planned as a 30-minute snatch: Delta operators arrest the targets, Rangers secure the perimeter, helicopters provide overwatch, a ground convoy extracts everyone. Instead, it became the longest sustained firefight involving American troops since Vietnam.

**What went wrong:** The raid launched at **3:32 PM in broad daylight** — negating SOF's night-operations advantage. After six prior raids using identical helicopter-insertion tactics, Aidid's militia had adapted: cell phones mobilized 2,000–4,000 fighters within minutes. PFC Todd Blackburn's 60-foot fall from a fast rope immediately diverted three vehicles. Then Super Six One was shot down by RPG at 4:15 PM, committing all rescue assets. Super Six Four went down 34 minutes later — with no resources left for a second crash site.

MSG Gary Gordon and SFC Randall Shughart volunteered to defend Super Six Four's crew, holding out approximately 45 minutes before being overrun and killed — **both received the Medal of Honor posthumously.** Pilot CW3 Michael Durant was captured.

The ground convoy carrying prisoners circled for 45 minutes unable to find the crash site through dust, smoke, and barricades. A multinational rescue convoy (100 vehicles, Malaysian APCs, Pakistani tanks) didn't depart until 11:24 PM and didn't reach the first crash site until 1:55 AM. Some soldiers had to run the "Mogadishu Mile" on foot during the dawn extraction.

**The critical resource denial:** MG Thomas Montgomery had requested M1 Abrams tanks, Bradley Fighting Vehicles, and **AC-130 Spectre gunships**. Secretary of Defense Les Aspin denied the request, citing the political sensitivity of an expanded military footprint. Mark Bowden later wrote there "likely would not have been a battle" had AC-130s been available.

**Casualties:** 18 Americans killed, 73–84 wounded, 1 POW (Durant, held 11 days). 2 Malaysian soldiers killed. Estimated 314–500+ Somalis killed. Three Black Hawks lost. Bodies of US soldiers were dragged through streets on live television.

**Consequences — strategic catastrophe:** Clinton ordered all combat operations terminated within three days and announced full withdrawal by March 1994. Aspin resigned. The debacle produced **Presidential Decision Directive 25**, setting restrictive criteria for future peacekeeping. It directly contributed to US inaction during the 1994 Rwandan genocide. Osama bin Laden later cited the withdrawal as proof of American weakness: "the weakness, feebleness and cowardliness of the U.S. soldier."

**Key sources:** Mark Bowden, *Black Hawk Down*; Clayton Chun, *Gothic Serpent* (Osprey); Senate Armed Services Committee Somalia report; Modern War Institute Urban Case Study #9

## 6. Roberts Ridge became a case study in cascading command failures (March 4, 2002)

**Units:** MAKO 30 (DEVGRU/SEAL Team 6 reconnaissance team), USAF Combat Controller TSgt John Chapman, 160th SOAR, Ranger QRF (1st Bn/75th Rangers) | **Location:** Takur Ghar mountain (10,469 ft), Shah-i-Kot Valley, Paktia Province, Afghanistan

**What happened:** During Operation Anaconda, SEAL Team 6's MAKO 30 was assigned to establish an observation post on Takur Ghar — a historic mujahideen position with fortified bunkers and a DShK heavy machine gun. The original plan called for offset insertion 1,400 meters from the peak. Multiple delays (a late B-52 sortie, an engine failure requiring helicopter swap) destroyed the timeline. With dawn approaching, Senior Chief Britt Slabinski made the fateful decision to **insert directly onto the peak** rather than abort. BG Gregory Trebon approved.

At 0245, Razor 03 was immediately hit by RPG and DShK fire. **PO1 Neil Roberts fell from the open ramp** into enemy territory. The helicopter crash-landed seven miles away. Predator drone footage later showed al-Qaeda fighters chasing and likely executing Roberts.

Razor 04 returned and reinserted MAKO 30 onto the peak. In the ensuing firefight, TSgt John Chapman was hit multiple times. Slabinski believed Chapman was dead and ordered retreat. **Predator footage analyzed years later revealed Chapman regained consciousness and fought alone against multiple al-Qaeda fighters, killing at least two, before being killed approximately 45 seconds before the Ranger QRF helicopter arrived.**

**The communications catastrophe:** Parallel, independent chains of command — JSOC's Task Force 11 and CJTF Mountain's conventional forces — failed to share critical information. The Ranger QRF at Bagram was launched with **false and outdated information**, told they were extracting a "SEAL sniper team in contact" when the SEALs had already withdrawn. Headquarters attempts to redirect Rangers to a safe offset landing zone were never received due to malfunctioning aircraft communications. Razor 01 flew directly into the hot LZ.

**Casualties:** 7 Americans killed — PO1 Neil Roberts (DEVGRU), TSgt John Chapman (USAF), SGT Philip Svitak (160th SOAR), CPL Matt Commons, SGT Brad Crose, SPC Marc Anderson (all 75th Rangers), SrA Jason Cunningham (USAF PJ). Twelve wounded. Two Chinooks destroyed or heavily damaged.

**The Chapman controversy:** The Air Force pushed for Chapman's Medal of Honor; the Navy contested the narrative. In 2018, both Chapman and Slabinski received the Medal of Honor — an unprecedented resolution that exposed deep inter-service tensions over what actually happened on that mountain.

**Key sources:** Malcolm MacPherson, *Roberts Ridge*; Pete Blaber, *The Mission, The Men, and Me*; Sean Naylor, "The Ghost in the Radio," *Newsweek*; LTC Andy Milani's classified investigation and Army War College paper

## 7. Pat Tillman's death revealed a systemic capacity for deception (April 22, 2004)

**Units:** 2nd Battalion, 75th Ranger Regiment | **Location:** Canyon near Spera, Khost Province, Afghanistan

**What happened:** CPL Pat Tillman — the former NFL safety who turned down a $3.6 million contract to enlist as a Ranger — was killed by three rounds to the head at less than 10 yards, fired by his own platoon mates in Serial 2 during a confused engagement in a narrow canyon. An Afghan militia soldier was also killed. **There were no enemy forces present when Tillman died.**

**The cover-up was deliberate and institutional.** Soldiers knew within minutes it was fratricide. Officers knew within hours. Yet Tillman's uniform and body armor were **burned**. His weapon and helmet were never recovered. The Army publicly claimed Tillman died in an enemy ambush and initiated a **Silver Star** for heroism under enemy fire — a citation that fabricated Taliban engagement entirely. The Silver Star was approved and signed by commanders including MG Stanley McChrystal, who knew within days the likely truth.

On April 29, McChrystal sent a classified P4 ("Personal For") memo warning that if the President or Secretary of the Army made public statements about Tillman's heroism, it "could cause public embarrassment if the circumstances of Corporal Tillman's death become public." The memo focused on **managing the political narrative**, not informing the family. LTG Philip Kensinger attended Tillman's nationally televised memorial service on May 3 knowing friendly fire was suspected. **The family was not told the truth until May 28 — five weeks later.**

**Investigations:** Seven investigations followed, including Army AR 15-6s, CID investigation, DoD Inspector General review, and House Committee on Oversight hearings chaired by Rep. Henry Waxman. Rumsfeld and senior generals testified with approximately **82 instances of "I don't recall."** Kevin Tillman told Congress: "The deception surrounding this case was an insult to the family; but more importantly, its primary purpose was to deceive a whole nation."

**Consequences:** LTG Kensinger received written reprimand and censure for submitting false reports and lying to investigators. McChrystal received **no punishment** — determined to have "reasonably and appropriately" relied on the Silver Star packet — and went on to command all US/NATO forces in Afghanistan. Seven enlisted Rangers received Article 15s. The Silver Star was modified but not revoked.

**Key sources:** Jon Krakauer, *Where Men Win Glory*; Mary Tillman, *Boots on the Ground by Dusk*; DoD IG Report (March 2007); House Report 110-858; *The Tillman Story* (documentary, 2010)

## 8. Operation Red Wings and the "Lone Survivor" myth (June 28, 2005)

**Units:** 4-man SEAL reconnaissance team (SDVT-1/SDVT-2), SEAL Team 10 QRF, 160th SOAR | **Location:** Sawtalo Sar mountain, Kunar Province, Afghanistan

**What happened:** A four-man SEAL team — LT Michael Murphy, PO2 Danny Dietz, PO2 Matthew Axelson, and HM2 Marcus Luttrell — was inserted by helicopter to conduct surveillance on local insurgent leader Ahmad Shah. Compromised by goat herders, the team was ambushed. Three SEALs were killed. A Chinook carrying the 16-man QRF was shot down by RPG, killing all aboard. **Nineteen Americans died** — the deadliest day for Naval Special Warfare since D-Day.

**What went wrong:** Intelligence estimated Shah's force at 8–10 fighters. The SEAL team was too small for the terrain (Marines had planned a six-man team). Helicopter insertion within one mile of a populated area ensured detection. The team had **inadequate communications capability** for the mountainous terrain — the known radio blackout problem was never resolved. LT Murphy exposed himself to enemy fire to make a satellite phone call for help, the act earning him a posthumous Medal of Honor.

The QRF response was catastrophic: two MH-47 Chinooks flew without Apache escort into the immediate target area. LCDR Erik Kristensen, who insisted on leading the QRF personally over pilot objections, was among the 16 killed when Turbine 33 was hit by an RPG.

**The contested narrative:** Marcus Luttrell's book *Lone Survivor* (2007) and the subsequent film created a widely accepted account now challenged by military historians. Ed Darack's *Victory Point* — based on Marine intelligence records — established that Shah's force numbered **8–10 fighters, not the 80–200 claimed by Luttrell.** Two propaganda videos shot by Shah's men during the firefight show only seven militants. Patrick Kinser, a Marine officer involved in Red Wings, called Luttrell's claims "exaggerated nonsense." Mohammad Gulab, the Afghan villager who rescued Luttrell, told *Newsweek* that Luttrell still had all 11 of his ammunition magazines when found. Former Marine Colonel Andrew MacMannis confirmed there were **"no reports of any enemy casualties"** when recovery teams combed the mountain.

**Key sources:** Ed Darack, *Victory Point* (definitive account); Luttrell, *Lone Survivor* (popular but contested); Ross Schneiderman, *Newsweek* (2016); Ed Darack, "Operation Red Wings: The (Mis)Information Aftermath," *Marine Corps Gazette*

## 9. MARSOC's first deployment ended in a wrongful disgrace (March 4, 2007)

**Units:** Fox Company, 2nd Marine Special Operations Battalion (MARSOC), "Task Force Violent" | **Location:** Shinwar District, Nangarhar Province, Afghanistan

**What happened:** Just 30 days into MARSOC's first-ever combat deployment, a 30-man platoon led by Maj. Fred Galvin was ambushed by a suicide VBIED and small arms fire in Bati Kot. As Marines fought through the complex ambush, Afghan officials alleged indiscriminate firing at civilians, initially claiming **19 civilians killed and 50 wounded.**

**What went wrong — but not what was claimed:** MG Frank Kearney ordered the entire 120-member unit expelled from Afghanistan. Galvin was relieved of command. Army Col. Nicholson publicly apologized, calling it **"a stain on our honor"** — before any investigation was completed. Compensation of $2,000 per family was paid to 50+ Afghan claimants.

A Court of Inquiry at Camp Lejeune in January 2008 found **no criminal wrongdoing** — the Marines acted appropriately under ambush. Actual casualties were 5–7 adult men killed and 2 civilians injured, not the 19+ initially claimed. But the Marines were never publicly exonerated for over a decade. Careers stagnated. Marriages failed. Galvin spent years fighting for vindication.

**Aftermath:** In 2018, the Marine Corps publicly affirmed full exoneration after lobbying by Rep. Walter Jones. In 2019, the Board for Correction of Naval Records recommended Galvin's retroactive promotion with back pay. Galvin published *A Few Bad Men* (2022), documenting how premature institutional panic and the rush to apologize destroyed careers of Marines who had done nothing wrong. The case became a cautionary tale about **command-level reflexive blame** before facts are established.

**Key sources:** Fred Galvin, *A Few Bad Men* (2022); MARSOC Court of Inquiry records (2008); Board for Correction of Naval Records decision (2019)

## 10. Linda Norgrove was killed by her rescuers, then the story was rewritten (October 8, 2010)

**Units:** DEVGRU (SEAL Team 6), 24 Army Rangers (75th Ranger Regiment) | **Location:** Dineshgal village, Korengal Valley, Kunar Province, Afghanistan

**What happened:** Linda Norgrove, a 36-year-old Scottish aid worker, was kidnapped on September 26, 2010. British PM Cameron authorized a US rescue after intelligence indicated she might be executed or moved to Pakistan. Twenty SEALs and 24 Rangers fast-roped from Chinooks onto near-vertical mountainside at 3:30 AM. During clearing operations, **a junior DEVGRU operator threw a fragmentation grenade into a depression between buildings** where he believed Taliban fighters were sheltering. Norgrove, wearing dark clothing, was in that depression with a captor. She was killed by fragmentation injuries to the head and chest.

**The cover-up:** NATO initially blamed a Taliban suicide vest. Days later, the team commander reviewed helmet camera footage and discovered the discrepancy. SEALs did not immediately notify senior officers about the grenade — breaching military law. A joint US-UK investigation (led by then-MG Joseph Votel and Brigadier Rob Nitsch) concluded Norgrove was killed by the US grenade. The operator was discharged from DEVGRU. A coroner exonerated him of criminal liability, finding he genuinely feared for his safety and was unaware of Norgrove's presence.

**Significance:** The case demonstrated how initial false narratives emerge rapidly in SOF operations and how the instinct to control the story can delay accountability. It also raised questions about the use of fragmentation grenades in hostage rescue operations.

**Key sources:** Joint US-UK Investigation Report (December 2010); British inquest proceedings; BBC and *Guardian* reporting

## 11. Extortion 17 was the deadliest day in SEAL history (August 6, 2011)

**Units:** 15 DEVGRU Gold Squadron operators, 2 NSW SEALs, 5 Naval Special Warfare support, 3 AFSOC, 5 Army National Guard aircrew, 7 Afghan commandos, 1 interpreter | **Location:** Tangi Valley, Wardak Province, Afghanistan

**What happened:** A 75th Ranger Regiment platoon raided a Taliban compound targeting a leader known as "Tahir." When fighters escaped, JSOC commanders assembled an Immediate Reaction Force to intercept the "squirters." Fifteen DEVGRU Gold Squadron operators and other SOF personnel loaded onto a conventional CH-47D Chinook (callsign Extortion 17) — not a special operations MH-47 variant with enhanced countermeasures.

At approximately 0239, flying at 250 feet and 70 mph into the narrow Tangi Valley, the helicopter was hit by one of three RPGs fired by two Taliban fighters positioned in a turret less than 150 yards from the landing zone. The round severed more than 10 feet of aft rotor blade. **All 38 aboard died** — making this the single deadliest incident for US forces in the entire Afghan war.

**What went wrong:** Despite a robust ISR package overhead — Predator drone, AC-130 gunship, Apache attack helicopters, specialized surveillance aircraft — **none identified the shooters before the engagement.** The landing zone had never been used before, yet fighters were pre-positioned nearby. An AC-130 fire control officer later claimed her crew spotted suspected Taliban moving toward the LZ and requested permission to fire but was denied. The mission was assembled rapidly for a relatively low-priority task: chasing fleeing Taliban fighters.

**Casualties:** 30 Americans and 8 Afghans killed. The RPG shooter was identified through intercepted radio boasts and killed two days later by an F-16 strike.

**Aftermath:** CENTCOM's investigation concluded all operational decisions were "tactically sound." Recommendations focused on improved sensor coverage, closer gunship escort, and enhanced LZ vetting for time-sensitive insertions. The incident fueled persistent conspiracy theories among some families — alleging Taliban foreknowledge or retaliation for the bin Laden raid — though no evidence supported these claims. (The Gold Squadron members killed were from a different squadron than the one that conducted the Abbottabad raid.)

**Key sources:** Ed Darack, *The Final Mission of Extortion 17* (Smithsonian Books, 2017); CENTCOM "Colt Report" (October 2011); House Oversight Subcommittee hearing (February 2014)

## 12. The Kunduz hospital strike killed 42 people SOF were supposed to protect (October 3, 2015)

**Units:** US Army Special Forces (ground element directing air support), USAF AC-130U gunship | **Location:** Kunduz city, Afghanistan

**What happened:** After Taliban forces seized Kunduz — the first major city to fall since 2001 — a US Special Forces team supporting the counteroffensive relayed target coordinates provided by Afghan forces to an AC-130U gunship. Starting at 2:08 AM, the gunship fired **211 shells over approximately one hour** into the Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) Kunduz Trauma Centre — the only specialized trauma facility in all of northeastern Afghanistan. MSF had shared the hospital's GPS coordinates with US DoD, Afghan ministries, and the US Army in Kabul, and received confirmation of receipt.

**What went wrong — a chain of at least eight compounding errors:** The AC-130 launched without crew receiving all required mission information. A critical communications system aboard had failed. The crew could not access the "No Strike List" containing the hospital's coordinates. Afghan forces provided coordinates for a different target entirely. When the JTAC relayed coordinates, the system directed the crew to an open field. Attempting visual identification based on verbal description, the crew **misidentified the brightly lit hospital as the Taliban target.** The attack continued for 30 minutes after MSF contacted US and Afghan officials reporting the hospital was being struck.

The US narrative shifted four times in four days: collateral damage, "tragic incident," strike requested by Afghan forces, then Gen. Campbell's admission that it was "a US decision, made in the US chain of command."

**Casualties:** **42 killed** — 24 patients, 14 MSF staff, 4 caretakers. 30+ injured. Patients burned alive in their beds. Staff were decapitated or lost limbs. Others were shot from the air while fleeing.

**Aftermath:** The DoD investigation found no war crime, attributing everything to human error and equipment failures. **Sixteen military personnel received administrative discipline only — no criminal charges.** Obama called MSF's president to apologize. The US made condolence payments of $6,000 per family killed. MSF called the attack a war crime and demanded an independent investigation; the US refused consent. The hospital was permanently closed.

**Key sources:** USCENTCOM 15-6 Investigation Report (April 2016); MSF Internal Review (November 2015); Human Rights Watch; UNAMA Special Report on Kunduz

## 13. The Tongo Tongo ambush exposed an invisible war gone wrong (October 4, 2017)

**Units:** ODA 3212, 3rd Special Forces Group (Airborne), ~11 US soldiers with ~30 Nigerien partners | **Location:** Near Tongo Tongo village, Tillabéri Region, Niger, near the Mali border

**What happened:** What began as a low-risk civil-military liaison patrol was redirected mid-mission into a **capture-or-kill operation** against ISIS in the Greater Sahara commander Doundou Chefou — without the personnel, equipment, or support required for such operations. After the target fled and the team recovered enemy equipment, they stopped at Tongo Tongo to resupply water. Departing around 11:40 AM, they were ambushed by approximately 100 ISGS fighters outnumbering the combined force roughly 3-to-1.

**What went wrong:** The mission change from routine patrol to combat operation bypassed adequate approval processes. No pre-mission rehearsals were conducted with Nigerien partners. **No overhead ISR** covered the team during the critical period — the surveillance asset had been redirected. No Quick Reaction Force was pre-positioned. Twenty-nine similar uneventful missions in the prior six months had created a false sense of security. The ISGS fighters had advance awareness of the team's presence.

**How the four Americans died:** SSG Bryan Black was killed instantly providing suppressive fire. SFC Jeremiah Johnson and SSG Dustin Wright were overrun fighting near the initial ambush site. SGT La David Johnson became separated during withdrawal and was killed approximately one mile from the main engagement. His body was not recovered for **48 hours**, fueling family anguish and public controversy.

Air support arrived too late: French Mirage jets appeared 100 minutes after the ambush began but couldn't drop ordnance due to ground confusion. Low-level passes scattered the militants. French helicopters later evacuated survivors.

**Political firestorm:** Trump did not publicly address the deaths for nearly two weeks. Rep. Frederica Wilson, in the car when Trump called La David Johnson's widow Myeshia, reported Trump said: **"He knew what he signed up for, but I guess it still hurts."** Myeshia Johnson confirmed on national television that Trump couldn't remember her husband's name. All four families reported receiving conflicting information about how their loved ones died.

**Aftermath:** AFRICOM's investigation (May 2018) found "individual, organizational, and institutional failures" but blamed no single cause. Three individuals were recommended for "appropriate action." Policy changes included authorization for heavily armored vehicles, overhauled mission-planning procedures, and armed US drone flights from Niger. Congress began scrutinizing the US military presence in Africa under the 2001 AUMF — many lawmakers were unaware the US had nearly 1,000 troops in Niger.

**Key sources:** AFRICOM Investigation Summary (May 2018); CSIS analysis; ABC News investigation by James Gordon Meek; PBS NewsHour and Military Times reporting

## 14. The Yakla raid was "a good example of what not to do" (January 29, 2017)

**Units:** DEVGRU (SEAL Team 6), UAE special operations commandos, CIA intelligence support | **Location:** Al-Ghayil village, Yakla, Al Bayda Governorate, Yemen

**What happened:** The first major counterterrorism raid of the Trump presidency — approved over dinner nine days into the administration — targeted an AQAP compound for intelligence gathering and possibly AQAP leader Qasim al-Rimi. The Obama administration had declined to authorize it, viewing it as escalation. **The approval did not follow the rigorous interagency vetting process** used by both the Bush and Obama administrations — no Principals Committee or Deputies Committee meeting was held.

**What went wrong:** The SEALs were detected on approach. An intense firefight erupted with AQAP fighters, including armed women. CPO William "Ryan" Owens was killed and three other SEALs wounded. An MV-22 Osprey ($75 million) suffered a hard landing and was destroyed by airstrike to prevent capture. Close air support devastated the village.

Civilian casualties were catastrophic and disputed: CENTCOM acknowledged "between 4 and 12" deaths; the Bureau of Investigative Journalism documented **25 civilians killed including 9 children under 13** — the youngest three months old. Among the dead was **8-year-old Nawar al-Awlaki**, an American citizen and daughter of AQAP cleric Anwar al-Awlaki (killed by US drone in 2011). Local tribal leaders insisted no AQAP leaders were present and that some men killed were actually government-aligned tribal commanders.

The primary target, al-Rimi, was not at the site. He later released an audio message mocking the raid, calling Trump "the fool of the White House." NBC News reported the raid **"yielded no significant intelligence"** despite White House claims of success.

**Political fallout:** Owens's father, himself a former special operator, refused to meet Trump at Dover AFB and demanded an investigation. Trump blamed "the generals." Yemen temporarily withdrew permission for US ground operations. International Crisis Group analyst April Longley Alley called it "a good example of what not to do" — a raid that ignored local political context and likely radicalized Yemeni tribesmen.

**Key sources:** NBC News investigations (Cynthia McFadden, William Arkin); *The Intercept* FOIA documents; Human Rights Watch; Bureau of Investigative Journalism; Gen. Votel's Senate Armed Services Committee testimony (March 2017)

## 15. SEAL Team 6's culture of impunity became its own institutional crisis (2002–2019)

**Units:** Naval Special Warfare Development Group (DEVGRU), all color-coded squadrons | **Location:** Afghanistan, Iraq, Yemen, and other theaters

**What happened:** Over nearly two decades, a documented pattern of war crimes, corpse mutilation, and institutional cover-up emerged within America's most elite counterterrorism unit. The pattern was first publicly exposed by Matthew Cole's landmark 2017 investigation in *The Intercept*, based on interviews with 18 current and former DEVGRU members, and expanded in the *New York Times'* Pulitzer-finalist series.

**Key documented incidents:**

- **"Canoeing" (systematic, ~2004–2011):** Operators routinely fired rounds into the foreheads of dead enemies at close range, splitting skulls into a V-shape. First noticed around 2004, the practice became widespread by 2007. Post-operation photographs were stored at DEVGRU headquarters. A former SEAL Team 6 leader stated: "There is and was no military reason whatsoever to split someone's skull open with a single round. It's sport."

- **March 2002, Shah-i-Kot Valley:** After the trauma of Roberts Ridge, DEVGRU Red Team operators struck a convoy based on tenuous Predator imagery. The targets turned out to be civilians. LCDR Vic Hyder allegedly shot an unarmed wounded survivor, then mutilated the corpse by stomping in the man's skull. Teammate Monty Heath reportedly said: "I'm morally flexible but I can't handle that."

- **Blue Squadron "kill everyone" guidance:** Master Chief Britt Slabinski allegedly gave pre-mission guidance that "every male at the target be killed." NCIS investigated a young SEAL observed slashing a dead fighter's throat; investigators concluded he "might have been cutting off gear." Despite substantiated allegations leading to his removal from DEVGRU, Slabinski was later promoted.

- **Eddie Gallagher case (2017–2019):** SOC Edward Gallagher of SEAL Team 7 was accused by his own platoon members of stabbing a wounded 17-year-old ISIS prisoner to death, shooting Iraqi civilians from a sniper position, and texting photos with the corpse captioned "got him with my hunting knife." At trial, a prosecution witness under immunity claimed he — not Gallagher — killed the prisoner. Gallagher was acquitted of murder, convicted only of posing for a photo with a corpse. **President Trump intervened repeatedly** — restoring Gallagher's rank, blocking a Trident Review Board, and firing Navy Secretary Richard Spencer.

- **Death of SSG Logan Melgar (June 2017, Mali):** An Army Green Beret was strangled to death during hazing by two DEVGRU members and two Marine Raiders. Criminal prosecutions followed.

**The oversight vacuum:** JSOC investigated JSOC. NCIS investigations were routinely blocked by the SEAL community. CIA paramilitary officer Richard Smethers, himself a retired SEAL Team 6 member, complained to CIA superiors about atrocities and threatened to go to the press. SEAL Team 6 told the CIA: "If this guy goes public, we will end his career." The CIA sent Smethers home. No complaint was filed. As one former SEAL Team 6 leader summarized the culture: **"You can't win an investigation on us. You don't whistleblow on the teams."**

**Consequences:** The Gallagher case exposed a generational rift — younger SEALs who reported misconduct ("The Sewing Circle") versus an old guard code of silence. Political intervention in military justice further eroded accountability. Congressional oversight remained minimal; State Department legal adviser Harold Koh noted Congress "notoriously doesn't want to know too much" about SEAL Team 6 activities.

**Key sources:** Matthew Cole, *Code Over Country: The Tragedy and Corruption of SEAL Team Six* (2022); Matthew Cole, "The Crimes of SEAL Team 6," *The Intercept* (January 2017); Mazzetti et al., "SEAL Team 6: A Secret History," *New York Times* (June 2015, George Polk Award winner); David Philipps, *Alpha: Eddie Gallagher and the War for the Soul of the Navy SEALs* (2021)

## What these 15 failures reveal about the enterprise

Across five decades, the same failure modes recur with striking consistency. **Intelligence failures** drove the Son Tay, Eagle Claw, Red Wings, Tongo Tongo, and Yakla disasters. **Inter-service rivalry** killed SEALs at Paitilla and drowned them at Grenada. **Parallel command structures** that refused to share information produced catastrophe at Mogadishu and Roberts Ridge. **Mission creep** — the quiet redefinition of objectives mid-operation — contributed directly to deaths in Niger, Panama, and Grenada. And **institutional narrative control** — the reflex to manage the story before managing accountability — corrupted the aftermath of Tillman, Norgrove, Kunduz, and the SEAL Team 6 investigations.

The consequential failures also share a paradox: each one drove institutional reform that made the next generation of operations better. Eagle Claw created USSOCOM. Grenada produced the Nunn-Cohen Amendment. Mogadishu transformed urban warfare doctrine. Roberts Ridge improved joint communications. Tongo Tongo overhauled African operations. But the SEAL Team 6 cultural crisis suggests that **operational excellence without moral accountability creates its own form of institutional failure** — one that no organizational restructuring can fix. That tension, between the demands of the mission and the demands of conscience, may be the defining challenge for the next era of American special operations.

A November 2024 GAO report quantified the broader toll: **3,600+ non-combat SOF accidents over a decade, 48 training deaths, 86% caused by human error.** SOCOM comprises just 3% of the joint force but has suffered over half of all US combat casualties in recent years. The enterprise built from the wreckage of these 15 failures now bears a disproportionate share of America's wars — and their consequences.
