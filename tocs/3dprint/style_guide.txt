VOICE AND TONE:
Write in first person to one specific 11-year-old reader. The author is a
friendly grown-up — think of a cool uncle who happens to run a business, or a
really good coach who's also smart about money. NOT a 20-year industry veteran
giving a mentor talk. NOT a businessperson talking to other businesspeople.
Use "I" freely. Tell stories from your own kid years. Make jokes the reader
will actually get. Be warm, curious, occasionally goofy, and always treat the
reader like a smart kid who can absolutely understand this if you explain it
right. They're 11, not 4. Never talk down.

TARGET AUDIENCE:
An 11-year-old reading the book by themselves. Curious, smart, but new to
3D printing. They watch YouTube and TikTok, play video games, go to school, have
some allowance money. They've heard of: Amazon, Apple, Disney, Nike, Lego,
Snickers, Coca-Cola, Roblox, Minecraft, iPhone, McDonald's, Walmart, Costco,
Target. They have NOT heard of (or won't recognize): slicer, filament, infill,
extrude, hotend, gantry, mesh, manifold, decimate, G-code, FDM, calibration,
CoreXY, build volume, retraction, adhesion, PLA, PETG, ABS, TPU. Some of these
the book will teach on purpose; the rest should just go away (see VOCABULARY).

READING LEVEL:
5th-to-6th-grade reading level. Short sentences for important points. Longer
sentences are okay when they flow naturally, but never write a long sentence
to sound smart — write a long sentence because the meaning genuinely needs to
unspool. Break up any paragraph that runs past four sentences. Use short
paragraphs. Sometimes a one-sentence paragraph is the strongest move in the
whole chapter.

VOCABULARY:
Real maker words are worth teaching at this age. The reader should finish
the book knowing what "filament," "layer," "slicer," and "infill" actually
mean — those are useful words a curious 11-year-old will hear again every time
they go near a 3D printer for the rest of their life. Other adult jargon adds
nothing and should just go away.

So there are two lists.

TEACH THESE WORDS — define on first use, then reinforce in parentheses on
later uses so the kid sees the word and the plain-English meaning side by
side until the word sticks:

  - filament ("the plastic string the printer melts to build with")
  - nozzle ("the tiny hole the melted plastic squirts out of")
  - layer ("one thin slice of the print, stacked on the one below it")
  - slicer ("the program that turns a 3D model into printer instructions")
  - infill ("the pattern inside a print — how full or hollow it is")
  - supports ("temporary scaffolding that holds up parts that would droop")
  - PLA ("the easy, friendly starter plastic, basically made from corn")
  - overhang ("a part that sticks out over nothing, with no layer to land on")

The pattern for these works like this:

  First exposure:  "...the pattern the printer fills the inside with —
                    grown-ups call this the 'infill.'"

  Second exposure: "...so we set a low infill (the pattern inside, mostly
                    empty air)."

  Third exposure:  "...the infill (the inside fill) is only ten percent."

  Later:           "crank the infill up to 100%" — by this point, no parens
                    needed; the reader knows the word.

Repeat the parenthetical reminder a couple of extra times across the book
even after the kid probably knows the word — better to slightly over-teach
than to lose them. Words like "the CANVAS system," "RFID chip," "Simplify /
decimate," "watertight (non-manifold)," "CoreXY," "brim," "purge / flushing,"
and "build plate" don't need this treatment because the TOC already gives each
of them a whole teaching moment.

REPLACE THESE WORDS — never use them at all; the kid-friendly version is
strictly better:

  - "extrude / extrusion" → "squirt the melted plastic out"
  - "hotend" → "the hot part that melts the plastic" (or just say "nozzle")
  - "FDM" → "this kind of 3D printer" (never use the acronym)
  - "G-code" → "the file of printer instructions" the first time, then just
    "the sliced file" — don't drill the term
  - "mesh" → "the model," or "a skin made of tiny triangles"
  - "non-manifold" → "not watertight" / "a model with holes that couldn't
    hold water" (use the software's word only once, in passing)
  - "decimate" → "simplify" or "throw away triangles" (that's the slicer's
    own button name anyway)
  - "STL / OBJ / 3MF" → just "model file" most of the time; name a format only
    when the kid actually has to pick one
  - "PEI / PEO / PET" (plate coatings) → "the coating on the build plate"
  - "adhesion" → "how well the first layer sticks"
  - "warping" → "curling up at the corners" — describe it, don't drill it
  - "calibration" → "the printer checking itself"
  - "gantry" → "the arm that moves the nozzle"
  - "mm/s" (spoken as jargon) → "millimeters a second — about as fast as you
    can swipe your hand across a table"
  - "retraction / flow rate / Z-hop / stringing / elephant's foot" → don't go
    there at all; this book doesn't tune these, so the words never appear

METAPHORS AND COMPARISONS:
Pull metaphors from a kid's actual life: the cafeteria, recess, the lunch
line, video games, sports practice, family dinner, allowance, sleepovers,
the playground, summer camp, birthday parties, the school bus. NOT: dinner
parties, marriage, mortgages, salaries, the office, board rooms, retirement,
business meetings, conferences. If the original example feels like a
grown-up reference, swap it for the kid-life version. Maker-specific images
that land well at this age and are already in the TOC: the hot glue gun,
plastic spaghetti, a Lego baseplate, a hollow chocolate Easter bunny,
construction scaffolding, a too-tall sandwich, a balloon that won't hold air,
and a drawing made of a million dots vs. way fewer. Reuse and extend these;
don't reach for a grown-up comparison when a kid one is right there.

ANECDOTES:
Always real. The strongest stories here are real printing moments — the first
print you watched on the floor like a movie, the time a print failed overnight
and you didn't catch it, the first time the colors swapped and you thought the
machine was broken, the print you were way too proud of. "When I was about your
age, I..." still works for stories from before printing. "A kid I know once..."
also works. AVOID stories that sound like a grown-up's workday — clients,
deadlines, supply chains, the office. If a real story has to come from grown-up
life, frame it so a kid can picture it: "I was waiting for a print to finish and
noticed..." not "A coworker sent me a file and..."

CONCRETE NUMBERS:
Use real numbers — kids actually like specific numbers. But always make scale
feel real. Bad: "the nozzle hits 350°C." Good: "350°C — hot enough to cook an
egg in about a second." Bad: "a 256mm build volume." Good: "256mm each way —
about the length of a school ruler." Bad: "a 0.4mm nozzle." Good: "0.4mm —
about as thick as four sheets of paper stacked up." Bad: "a 200MB file." Good:
"200 megabytes, when the printer really wants a file more like a few — so it's
about fifty times too big." Bad: "0.2mm layers." Good: "0.2mm — thinner than
three of your hairs stacked up."

HUMOR:
Light, kid-appropriate. Wry observations about how weird 3D printing is. ("Yes,
the printer really does poop out little rainbow blobs. No, I am not making it
up.") Self-deprecating jokes about the author's own mistakes (the print you let
run for six hours that was doomed from layer one, the time you cranked every
setting up "to be safe" and wasted a whole afternoon). A little goofy is great.
Never punch down at any group — grown-ups, kids, beginners, anyone.

STRUCTURE:
Use the descriptive subheadings from the TOC — never generic ones. Each
chapter should feel like one solid hangout, not a manual. The reader should
finish each chapter thinking, "Wait, that's so obvious now, why doesn't
everyone see that?"

TRY-THIS EXERCISES:
Every chapter ends with a "Try This" section straight from the TOC. Write it
as a short, doable challenge, not homework. Keep it to a few clear steps in
plain language, and always tie it back to something the kid can see or feel —
the weight of a hollow print, the look of a good first layer, the difference
between two settings. Some exercises need the printer; some (especially early)
don't, and that's on purpose so a kid can start before the machine is even set
up. End the exercise on the payoff, not the chore: "...and now you'll never be
surprised by it again." When an exercise involves the printer running, assume
the safety rules from Part One are already in place — don't re-lecture.

KEEP IT REAL AND FOLLOWABLE:
This is a how-to the kid will actually follow at the machine, so the steps have
to be true, in order, and doable. Use the real tool and button names from the
TOC (OrcaSlicer's "Simplify Model," "Fix Model," the paint tool, "Height
Range," the Prepare and Preview tabs; the printer's CANVAS system, auto-level,
SD-card workflow). Never invent a step or a menu that doesn't exist. When a
real step is fiddly, say so honestly and give the trick — don't pretend it's
one tap when it isn't. If something genuinely varies between setups, say "it
might look a little different on yours, but you're looking for..." rather than
faking certainty.

SAFETY, HANDLED RIGHT:
The hot parts are real, but the tone is calm and matter-of-fact, never scary.
Frame safety as the short list of rules that lets a grown-up say yes to the kid
using this alone — three rules, then you basically never think about them again.
Confess a real (mild) burn or mistake to make the rules believable rather than
preachy. After Part One, don't keep re-warning; trust that the reader learned
it, and only mention heat or moving parts again when a new step genuinely
introduces a new risk.

DEFAULT WORD COUNT:
1,200 words per chapter unless otherwise specified. Lean shorter rather than
longer. Tight beats thorough at this age.

LEAN INTO:
- Direct address: "Here's something nobody's going to tell you..."
- The surprise reveal — the boring thing is secretly the best thing (the
  hollow inside is the smart move; the preview tab is the real superpower)
- Lists of three. Fast.
- "When I was your age" stories, and real printing-fail stories
- The satisfying moments — peeling a print off the magnet bed, snapping off
  the supports, the first layer going down clean
- Inviting the reader to test it themselves — every chapter should leave the
  kid wanting to go look at, weigh, slice, or print something and check
- Confessing when something is a little weird or wasteful ("I know throwing
  away that much plastic seems dumb. It kind of is. But here's why it happens.")

AVOID:
- Talking down to the reader
- Over-defining (one clear definition is plenty)
- Big words used to sound smart
- Clichés ("at the end of the day," "game-changer," "the bottom line,"
  "moving the needle")
- References to having a job, paying rent, getting a salary, retirement,
  having a mortgage, dating, marriage, going to college
- Deep tuning jargon (retraction, flow rate, temperature towers) — this book
  stops at the settings that actually matter
- Anything that assumes the reader has used a 3D printer or design software
  before
