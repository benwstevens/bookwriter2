# Style Guide — How Do You Make a Doctor?

This guide governs how the chapters of *How Do You Make a Doctor?* should be
written. It's the second book in a series for the same kid (Ben's 11-year-old
son), so the voice, audience, and reading level carry over from
*How Many Businesses?* unchanged. What's different here is the subject — medicine
instead of business — so the vocabulary list, the example-pool, and a few
new instructions about handling heavy material are tuned to this book.

If you've read the first book's style guide: most of this is the same.
The medicine-specific stuff is in sections 4, 6, and 7.

---

## 1. Audience and Voice

You are writing to ONE 11-year-old reader. Not "kids," not "young readers."
One specific kid, sitting on the other side of the page. He's smart, curious,
and trusts you, and he has just watched *The Pitt* with his dad and started
asking real questions about how a doctor gets made.

The narrator is a friendly grown-up — a cool uncle, an older cousin, a family
friend who happens to know a lot about this world. The narrator is in the same
room as the reader, walking next to him, pointing things out. First person.
Direct address. "You and I are going to..." "Picture this scene with me..."
"Here's something nobody tells you..."

What this voice is NOT:
- Not a textbook. No "In this chapter we will examine..."
- Not a teacher at the front of a classroom.
- Not condescending. The reader is 11, not 5.
- Not falsely cheerful. Don't oversell things.
- Not hedged. Be confident and direct.

Warm, direct, sometimes funny, sometimes serious. Treat the reader as
intelligent and capable of handling the truth.

---

## 2. Reading Level and Sentence Mechanics

Target reading level: roughly 5th-6th grade. That means:

- Short sentences mixed with medium ones. Avoid long, comma-heavy sentences
  that pile clauses on each other.
- Common words over uncommon ones when both work. "Used" is better than
  "utilized." "Stop" is better than "cease."
- Active voice almost always. Not "The patient was examined by the doctor" —
  "The doctor examined the patient."
- Contractions are fine and encouraged. "Doesn't," "it's," "you'll." The
  voice should sound spoken, not formal.
- One idea per sentence, mostly. Let the rhythm breathe.

Paragraphs should usually be 2–5 sentences. If a paragraph is running long,
break it. The page should feel inviting, not dense.

It's fine — and good — to use a one-sentence paragraph for emphasis. The
ceremony was over. The real work started Monday.

---

## 3. Word Count and Length

Default target per chapter: **1,200 words.** A few chapters in this book are
flagged as 1,300 or 1,400 in the TOC — those are intentional and should hit
their targets. Don't pad. Don't run short either.

What "1,200 words" looks like in practice: a careful intro (maybe 150–200
words), three to four chapter sections of roughly 200–250 words each, a
short conclusion (60–100 words), and a transition to the next chapter
(60–100 words). The intro and the bullets do most of the heavy lifting.

---

## 4. Vocabulary — Teach vs. Replace

Some medical terms are load-bearing for the book — the reader is going to
hear them again, and learning them is part of the point. Others are just
jargon, and should be paraphrased.

**TEACH these words** (introduce, define in plain language the first time,
then use freely):

- diagnosis, symptom, specialty, rotation, residency, attending, resident,
  intern, fellow, the Match, board exam, the wards, the OR, cadaver, surgery,
  pathology, anatomy, the USMLE, Step 1 / Step 2, ER, ICU

Define each ONCE, the first time it appears in the book. A natural definition
inside a sentence is better than a dictionary-style aside. "A resident — a
doctor who already finished med school but is still being trained..."

**REPLACE or paraphrase these** (they're jargon and the reader doesn't need
them):

- differential diagnosis → "the list of things it could be"
- etiology → "what caused it"
- pathognomonic → just don't use it
- morbidity / mortality → "how often it hurts people" / "how often it kills"
- pre-rounding → "showing up early to look at all your patients"
- gunner → "the student who's trying way too hard"
- scut → use the word, but explain it ("the boring jobs nobody wants")
- pimping → call it "getting grilled" (more on this below)
- post-call / call schedule → "the day after your overnight shift"

**Handle carefully** — load-bearing but sensitive:

- *Pimping* is the real medical-education term for being grilled with hard
  questions in front of the team. The word is used inside medicine and
  shouldn't be repeated in a kids' book. Use "getting grilled" or "being
  put on the spot" throughout. Briefly note that doctors have their own
  word for it but we're not going to use that one here. Don't make a big
  deal of it.
- *Cadaver* is fine to use, and the cadaver lab chapter uses it freely.
  The respectful framing in the TOC does the work.

---

## 5. Metaphors and Examples — Kid-Life Only

Every analogy and example should come from the world of an 11-year-old.
This is the single biggest stylistic rule.

**Use:** Minecraft, Pokémon, Hogwarts, video games in general, sports
practice and tryouts, sleepaway camp, school field trips, the cafeteria, the
school bus, science class, recess, kids' birthday parties, learning an
instrument, the Titanic, the playground, Legos, board games, summer break.

**Do NOT use:** marriage, divorce, mortgages, salaries, 401(k)s, dating,
office politics, corporate ladders, commuting, taxes, bills. The reader
doesn't live in any of those worlds yet. (One exception: when explaining
the burnout chapter, you can briefly reference grown-up things like
paperwork and insurance — the chapter is *about* those things, and the
reader's curiosity will carry him through.)

When you reach for an analogy, ask: would my 11-year-old reader feel this
in his own life? If yes, use it. If no, find something else.

The TOC already proposes specific analogies for most chapters
(Minecraft = fire-hose memorization, Pokémon = pathology, Hogwarts =
specialty tour, sports tryouts = sub-Is). Use the one the TOC proposes
when it's there. If you have a better one, swap it — but check first.

---

## 6. Anecdotes and Sources — Honest About What You Know

This book has one big difference from *How Many Businesses?*: the
narrator (Ben) actually ran businesses. He didn't go to med school.
That changes how anecdotes work in this book.

**What you can do freely:**

- Use real, specific, true things that happen in medicine. Libby Zion. The
  2003 work-hours rule. The white coat ceremony (1990s, Arnold P. Gold).
  The 80-hour week. The 2026 Match numbers. The $223,000 average debt.
  The 42% burnout rate. Real specific stuff.
- Use scenes from rotations the way doctors describe them in memoirs,
  podcasts, and online (r/medicalschool, r/Residency). The "drinking from a
  fire hose" line. The first time a code goes well. The intern who calls a
  nurse's question wrong and learns the right way. These are real, even
  if no one specific person owns them.
- Use composite anecdotes — "a med student I knew once told me..." or
  "a friend who's a surgeon says..." — when the underlying experience is
  universal among doctors. These should be true to what really happens,
  even if the specific friend is composite.

**What to avoid:**

- Don't invent a fake first-person medical career. The narrator hasn't
  done these things. He's heard about them, read about them, talked to
  doctors about them.
- Don't pretend to know things only insiders know unless you've sourced it
  to the kind of place a reader would believe (a study, a doctor's memoir,
  a specific hospital story).
- When you don't know something, it's fine to say so. "I'm not a doctor, but
  I've talked to enough of them that they all say the same thing about
  July 1st..." This is a strength, not a weakness — it tells the reader
  the narrator is honest about his sources.

The voice should still feel close to this world — research-close, not
lived-close.

---

## 7. Handling Heavy Material

This book has heavier moments than the business book did. There are
chapters or sections about death, burnout, suicide-by-overwork (Libby Zion),
malpractice, and the moral weight of caring for sick people. These have to
be handled honestly without traumatizing an 11-year-old. The rules:

1. **Tell the truth. Don't soften it into a lie.** When the cadaver lab
   chapter says "a real person, who used to walk around and eat dinner
   and have a family" — that's the right kind of honesty. Don't dodge.

2. **Don't linger in the suffering for effect.** Get to what it means and
   move on. Libby Zion's death gets one paragraph that names what happened
   and one paragraph about what changed because of it. Then we're out.
   Same instinct a good parent uses telling a kid about something hard
   in real life: name it, contextualize it, move forward.

3. **Frame the heaviness as part of why the job is meaningful, not just
   sad.** The hard parts of being a doctor — the patient who dies, the
   exhausted intern at 3am, the OB/GYN at the wrong end of a lawsuit — are
   what make the good parts mean what they mean. The book should feel
   serious in those spots, not grim.

4. **No graphic detail.** Cadaver lab is described in terms of teamwork
   and respect, not what a body looks like 8 weeks into dissection. Death
   on the wards is talked about as a moment of weight, not a moment of
   gore. The reader can imagine plenty on his own; the narrator stays one
   click less graphic than the reader's imagination.

5. **No content that could land badly with a kid.** Don't describe specific
   suicide methods, even Libby-Zion-adjacent. Don't dwell on physician
   suicide as a statistic without framing. If you're not sure whether a
   detail crosses the line, leave it out. The book doesn't need it.

---

## 8. Numbers and Specifics — Make Them Tangible

Real numbers should appear constantly, and they should always be made
tangible to an 11-year-old's life. "$223,000 in debt" by itself doesn't
land. "$223,000 in debt — that's more than what most families pay for
their house" does.

Some examples of the move:

- "About 53,000 people applied for about 44,000 residency spots in the
  2026 Match. That means roughly 1 in 5 applicants who put in a full
  effort didn't match. Imagine your soccer tryouts but with that many
  kids competing."

- "Residents make around $68,000 a year. Sounds like a lot for a kid.
  Now imagine making it while you owe $223,000 in loans and you're
  working 80 hours a week. The math doesn't really feel like a lot
  anymore."

- "Surgeries can last 4, 8, sometimes 12 hours. That's like standing still
  through three back-to-back baseball games, except you can't sit down,
  can't eat, can't go to the bathroom, and someone's life is happening
  on the table in front of you."

Concrete is always better than abstract. Specific is always better than
general. "A drug called formaldehyde, which keeps things from rotting"
beats "preservatives." "The famous program called Anki" beats "a
flashcard app."

## 9. The Chapter Shape

Each chapter should follow the structure laid out in the TOC:

- **Intro** (150–200 words): The opening device specified in the TOC —
  usually a scene, a freeze-frame, or a specific analogy. Don't drift.
  The intro should hook the reader and set up the chapter's central
  question or image.

- **Chapter sections** (3–5 of them, ~200–250 words each): Each one has a
  heading, a sentence or two of framing, and then content that delivers
  on the bullet points in the TOC. The bullets in the TOC are not a
  checklist to march through — they're the points the section must hit,
  in whatever order reads best.

- **Conclusion** (60–100 words): A synthesis. What does the reader
  understand at the end that they didn't at the start? Not a recap.

- **Transition** (60–100 words): A thematic bridge to the next chapter.
  Reference what comes next by theme, not just by title.

Headings should be specific and a little punchy — "The Twelve-Year
Sentence," "Bottom Of The Totem Pole," "The Day You Either Fall In Love
Or Run." Not "Section 1" or "Main Points."

---

## 10. The "Insider Truth" Bullet

Most chapters in the TOC include at least one bullet that delivers the
kind of thing an industry veteran would say — the unwritten rule, the
political reality, the thing everyone in the room understands but doesn't
say out loud. These are the bullets that make the book feel real.

When you write the chapter, make sure those bullets actually land. Don't
soften them. Don't generalize them. If the bullet says "most surgeons,
underneath the bluntness, are protective of their patients in an almost
grandparent-y way" — keep the specificity. The voice can be playful
about delivering them: "Here's something nobody tells you..." "Here's
the secret..." "Most doctors won't say this out loud, but..."

These are also the moments where the narrator is allowed to have a
strong opinion. Use it.

---

## 11. What to Avoid

A short list of things that break the voice:

- "It's important to remember that..." — never use this phrase.
- "As we'll see in the next chapter..." — never use this phrase outside
  the transition section.
- Over-explaining a joke or analogy. Trust the reader.
- Stacks of qualifiers. "It might be possible that some doctors sometimes
  feel..." Just say the thing.
- Stacked rhetorical questions. One is fine; three in a row feels gimmicky.
- Lecture-y connective tissue. "Now, let's turn our attention to..."
- Emoji.
- Lists with bullets inside the chapter text. The TOC has bullets in the
  outline so YOU know what to cover — the chapter itself should be prose.
- Talking down to the reader. He's 11, not 5. "You probably don't know
  this yet, but..." is bad. Just say the thing.

---

## 12. The Final Sanity Check

When a chapter is done, read it out loud as if you're reading it TO an
11-year-old. If you stumble, if a sentence feels stiff, if a paragraph
is too long, if the metaphor wouldn't land — fix it. The book should
sound like someone talking, not someone writing.

And one last check: does this chapter make the reader want to read the
next one? If yes, the transition did its job. If no, the transition needs
work.

That's the whole guide. Go make a great chapter.
