# Style guide template. The model fills the <angle-bracket> lines from the
# setup questionnaire (README, Part B1 round 6) and saves the result as
# style_guides/default.txt. Lines starting with # are notes and are removed.
# The READABILITY block is the fixed skeleton; keep it unless the person
# deliberately loosens or tightens a line in round 6f.

WHO IS SPEAKING:
<The author in one or two sentences: who they are, how long they have done
this, why they are the one to explain it. First person or third person.>

WHO IS READING:
<The reader: who they are, what they already know, what they do not, and
where they read (phone, bed, desk, a parking lot before doing the thing).
If there is a reader pair, say who the second reader is and how the text
speaks to them.>

VOICE AND TONE:
<The register in a phrase, then two sentences of how it behaves: a mentor
over coffee; a smart friend at dinner; a mischievous guide building the
thing alongside you; a plain explainer. What it never does: lecture, talk
down, gush.>

READING LEVEL:
<Grade level. Tenth grade is the default for adults; eighth for a phone
reader in a hurry; seventh to eighth for a kid with a grown-up alongside.>
Big words are welcome if defined once, in plain language, the first time
they appear. Prefer the plain word when one exists.

READABILITY (the skeleton; the secret of a book people finish):
- Chapters of about 1,300 words and never more than 1,500.
- No sentence longer than 25 words.
- No paragraph longer than 200 words, and rarely more than five sentences.
- Three to five body sections of four to five paragraphs each, with
  descriptive subheadings, never "Section 1".
- An opener of two to three paragraphs that earns the reader's attention
  with a specific device: an analogy from another field, a scene, a fact.
- A conclusion of one to two paragraphs: what the reader now understands,
  and the caveat everyone in the field knows.
- An "in the next chapter" teaser of a few sentences with a note of tension.

STORIES:
<Real stories only, or composites allowed if flagged? Whose stories: the
author's own, named people, unnamed cases? How many per chapter?>

METAPHORS:
<Where they come from (everyday life; a field far from the subject; never
the subject itself). Whether every metaphor must say where it breaks down.>
Avoid clichés ("at the end of the day," "it's a marathon, not a sprint").

NUMBERS AND EVIDENCE:
<Whether big numbers are anchored to something physical. Whether claims are
labeled (well established / contested / plausible but unmeasured). Whether
sources are named in the sentence. How the unknown is handled: said plainly,
never papered over.>

HUMOR:
<What kind and how often. What it is never at the expense of.>

BANNED:
<Words, devices, and habits the author never wants: em-dashes; hype words
("leverage," "robust," "journey," "game-changing," "delve"); filler ("it is
important to note"); equations; anything the reader would need a calculator
for. One per line.>

RECURRING ELEMENTS:
<Any fixed pieces every chapter carries, or "none": a sidebar for a second
reader; a "try this" task; a myth box; a one-line recap of where the book
stands.>

STRUCTURE:
Follow the chapter structure in the outline; its sections become the body's
subheadings, in order. Stay within 10 percent of each chapter's word target.
