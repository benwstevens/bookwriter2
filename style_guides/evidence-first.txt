VOICE AND TONE:
First person. The author is a careful reader, not an expert, who got tired of
contradictory advice on the subject and went and found what had actually been
measured. The tone is a smart friend handing you the short version of
something they had to dig for. Direct, warm, lightly wry. Never guru-ish,
never breathless.

THE CENTRAL PROMISE, WHICH EVERY CHAPTER MUST HONOR:
This book only tells you things that were measured. When something has not
been measured, the book says so out loud rather than filling the space with an
opinion. That honesty is the product. A chapter that slides into the usual
advice-giving voice has failed.

TARGET AUDIENCE:
An adult beginner. Assume intelligence, assume no vocabulary. Every technical
term gets defined the first time it appears, in one clause, and then gets used
normally.

READING LEVEL:
Eighth grade. Short sentences. One idea per sentence. Break any paragraph
that runs past four sentences. This is a book someone reads on a phone in a
parking lot before doing the thing.

EVIDENCE HANDLING, THE MOST IMPORTANT SECTION OF THIS GUIDE:
Every factual claim carries its weight in plain language, using three labels
consistently, in ordinary prose, not as badges:
  - Well established. Measured repeatedly. Say it flatly.
  - Contested. Real researchers disagree. Give both sides in two sentences
    and do not resolve it.
  - Plausible but unmeasured. Widely taught, nobody has checked. Say exactly
    that, then say what you would do in the meantime and why.
Numbers get a source in the sentence, not a footnote. Never quote a figure
from a popular book as if it came from a study. If a famous number turns out
to have no primary source, that is worth a paragraph, not a deletion.

PRIORITIZATION IS THE SPINE:
The book exists because the usual advice gives you thirty things and no
order. Every chapter says where its subject sits in the order, and does not
smuggle in extra things to work on. A chapter that leaves the reader holding
a longer list than when they started has failed.

READABILITY (the skeleton; the secret of a book people finish):
- Chapters of about 1,300 words and never more than 1,500 (a shorter target
  in the outline wins).
- No sentence longer than 25 words.
- No paragraph longer than 200 words, and rarely more than five sentences.
- Three to five body sections of four to five paragraphs each, with
  descriptive subheadings.
- An opener of two to three paragraphs built on a specific device.
- A conclusion of one to two paragraphs, then an "in the next chapter"
  teaser of a few sentences with a note of tension.

STRUCTURE AND LENGTH:
1,000 words per chapter unless the outline says otherwise. Short chapters,
one idea each. Descriptive subheadings. Every chapter ends with the one thing
the reader should go do, and how they would know it worked.

METAPHORS:
Pull them from outside the subject: cooking, carpentry, driving, learning an
instrument. Avoid clichés and avoid war and battle metaphors.

WHAT TO AVOID:
Motivational filler. "Trust the process." Invented anecdotes about named
people. Product recommendations by brand. Pretending a hard thing is easy, or
an easy thing is deep.
