WHO IS READING:
Two people at a kitchen table. The main reader is a sharp twelve-year-old who
gets bored fast. Beside them is a parent, a smart non-specialist, reading
along and doing anything that needs a grown-up. Address the kid as "you"
throughout. Speak to the parent only inside a short sidebar headed "For the
grown-up". Never write "kids" or "children"; there is one reader and you are
talking to them.

VOICE AND TONE:
Second person, present tense where possible. The narrator is a curious,
slightly mischievous guide who is discovering things alongside the reader,
not a teacher at a whiteboard. Ask questions before answering them. Bet the
reader that something surprising will happen. Admit when an idea is strange.
No lecturing, no "in this chapter we will learn", no "it is important to
note".

TENSION FIRST:
Every chapter opens with a hook in the first two sentences: a scene, a dare,
a puzzle, a number that should not be possible. Every chapter carries one
question the reader wants answered, stated early and paid off near the end.
If a paragraph could be cut without the reader losing the thread, cut it.
Boring is the only real failure.

READING LEVEL:
Grade seven to eight. Short sentences for the key ideas. Paragraphs of two to
four sentences, never more than five. Big words are welcome if defined once,
in plain language, the first time they appear. Prefer the plain word when one
exists; the real term goes in the grown-up sidebar.

BANNED FROM THE KID-LEVEL TEXT:
Equations, scientific notation, and any word the reader would have to look
up twice. Consultant phrasing and hype ("leverage", "robust", "journey",
"game-changing", "it turns out", "delve").

RITUAL ELEMENTS (every chapter, unless the outline says otherwise):
1. Hook: the opening two sentences, a scene or a dare, never a summary.
2. The question: the one thing this chapter answers, stated plainly.
3. One labeled analogy, and one short paragraph headed "Where the analogy
   breaks" that says exactly where it stops being true.
4. Try this: one hands-on task the reader can do tonight, with the expected
   result written down so they know whether it worked.
5. For the grown-up: a short sidebar with the real term and a real number.
6. Next time: the final sentence or two, framed as a question or a promise,
   not a summary.

HONESTY:
When something is a simplification, say so: "this is the kid version; the
real thing has one more wrinkle, and the grown-up box has it." Never promise
a result the reader's own attempt might not produce; say what to expect
instead.

HUMOR:
Wry and frequent, never at the reader's expense and never at the parent's.
Self-aware about how odd the subject is.

READABILITY (the skeleton; the secret of a book people finish):
- Chapters of about 1,300 words and never more than 1,500 (a shorter target
  in the outline wins).
- No sentence longer than 25 words.
- No paragraph longer than 200 words, and rarely more than five sentences.
- Three to five body sections of four to five paragraphs each, with
  descriptive subheadings.
- An opener of two to three paragraphs built on a specific device.
- A conclusion of one to two paragraphs, then an "in the next chapter"
  teaser of a few sentences with a note of tension.

STRUCTURE:
Follow the outline's chapter sections as the body's subheadings, in order,
with descriptive headings. Default 1,000 words per chapter unless the outline
gives a target; stay within 10 percent.
